# 16. Creativity When AI Can Generate Everything

Researchers ran an experiment that should settle an argument and instead made it more interesting.

Hundreds of people were asked to write a short story. Some were given no help. Some were given one idea
from a language model. Some could choose from up to five.

The writers with AI help did better, and not marginally. Their stories were rated **more creative, better
written, and more enjoyable** — and the gains were largest for the writers who had scored *lowest* on a
creativity test beforehand.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11244532/ -->

Then came the part that changes what the result means. The AI-assisted stories were **more similar to each
other** than the stories written without it.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11244532/ -->

The researchers called it a social dilemma, and that is exactly right: with generative AI, each writer is
individually better off, and collectively a narrower range of work gets made.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11244532/ -->

> **The one thing to take away:** generating is now free, so generating is not the scarce part of
> creativity any more. What is scarce is choosing — and the choices are the part a generator cannot make
> for you.

## The dilemma is the finding

Most writing about AI and creativity picks a side. Either the machine is a muse and everyone is about to
become an artist, or the machine is a thief and nothing it makes counts.

The experiment above refuses both, and the refusal is the useful part.

Read the two results together and they are not in tension. They are the same effect seen from two positions.
Stand where one writer stands and AI is a real improvement — better stories, faster, and a particular lift
for people who were struggling. Stand where the *body* of writers stands and the total range of what gets
written has narrowed, because everyone is drawing from the same well.

That is a social dilemma in the technical sense, and it has the structure that makes social dilemmas hard:
**the individually rational move is the move that degrades the commons.**

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11244532/ -->

The authors are careful not to overstate it. It is one task, one model, one experiment.

But the shape is worth taking seriously, because it inverts the question this chapter's title asks. Everyone
is asking whether AI can be creative. The experiment suggests the more immediate question is what happens to
creativity — the shared, collective kind — when it is easy for everyone.

And notice what the dilemma does *not* say. It does not say stop using AI, and the chapter does not say that
either. It says the value moved. When a tool makes the average output better and the whole distribution
narrower, the thing still worth doing by hand is whatever keeps you off the average.

## Why choosing is the scarce part

Before the evidence about what people pay for, it is worth being precise about the mechanism, because
"choosing" sounds soft until you see why a generator structurally cannot do it.

A generative model is, in the plainest terms, a sampler. It has absorbed an enormous body of what has been
made, and it produces new material by drawing from that. This is not a criticism — it is what makes it
useful, and it is why it can produce twenty competent versions of almost anything.

But sampling has a property that matters enormously here. Ask for many samples and you do not get the
extremes; you get the centre. The more options a model produces, the more they cluster around what is
typical of its training, because that is what is most probable.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11244532/ -->

Now look at what "picking the best of five AI suggestions" actually is. It is selecting the most
representative point from that distribution.

It feels like a creative choice, and structurally it is the opposite: it is a move toward the centre. That
is the mechanism behind the experiment's second result, and it explains why the narrowing happens without
anyone intending it.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11244532/ -->

A real choice is different in kind. It brings in information that was not in the distribution at all: your
specific situation, the constraint the client actually has, the thing you noticed that nobody asked about,
the reason this should not look like the last one. None of that is in the training data, because it is about
*now* and about *you*.

So the practical test for whether you are choosing or sampling is not "did I pick one". It is: **could the
model have produced the option I chose, if I had asked it for more?**

If yes, you sampled. If the option only exists because of something you brought — a fact, a constraint, an
intention — you chose.

This also explains why the fix is not to avoid the tool. The tool is excellent at producing the twenty
versions you would have taken a week to reach. The scarce thing is whatever you add that changes where the
versions come from, and that is a small, specific, personal input rather than a large effort.

## What people are actually paying for

If quality were the whole story, the rest of this chapter would be short: use the tool, get better work,
move on.

The second body of research says quality is not the whole story, and it isolates the reason with an
unusually clean design.

Take a set of paintings that were all made by AI. Show them to people. But before showing them, randomly
label some as "human-created" and others as "AI-created".

People rated the "human" ones higher — on liking, on beauty, on profundity, and on worth.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10319694/ -->

Same images. Same quality. The label moved the judgement.

And the study went further, testing which ratings explained the effect, and found two: **perceived effort**,
and **narrativity** — whether the work seemed to tell a story about a person.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10319694/ -->

A third line of work found the same asymmetry with a different method: the products of AI are judged worse
than human-made work **even when their quality is comparable**.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1509974/full -->

Put those together and the thing people are buying is not the artefact. It is **evidence that someone was
there** — that a person spent something on it, and that there is a story behind it that is about a life
rather than about a prompt.

That has a consequence that cuts against the grain of how most people now work, and it is worth saying
plainly: **if you use a tool to remove all visible trace of effort, you may be removing the thing the buyer
was paying for.**

Not always, and not for every market — a legal summary has no such premium. But for anything where people
choose you rather than a competitor on grounds other than price, the effort is part of the product, and
hiding it is not efficiency. It is throwing away value you already paid for.

Which reframes what "creative work" is. It is not the output.

It is the output plus the record that a person made it and made choices along the way. The record can be
thin — a note on method, a mention of what you rejected, showing a draft. It does not need to be a
performance. It needs to exist.

## The part that is not about the output at all

Now the finding that makes this chapter's title honest, because everything above is still about the thing
you sell.

In a study of **7,182 people** in the UK, people who took part in arts and crafts reported higher happiness,
higher life satisfaction, and a stronger sense that their life is worthwhile.

The size is the surprising part: the boost to "life is worthwhile" was **as large as the boost from being in
employment**. And the authors were careful about what that does and does not mean, noting that the study is
correlational and that they cannot say crafting caused it.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1052289 -->

Sit with what that says about a chapter called "creativity when AI can generate everything".

If making things is partly a way of producing a sense that your life is worthwhile, then the value of making
was never only in the made thing. It was partly in the making.

That is why the framing of "the output is the point" gets strange when generation is free. If the output was
the point, and output is now cheap, then the activity should be close to worthless. But the evidence says
the activity still does something for the person doing it, and the research on what buyers pay for says it
does something for them too.

There is a smaller, commercial version of the same finding, and it is well-established enough to have a
nickname. The **handmade effect** — the finding that labelling a product "handmade" raises what people will
pay for it — holds even when the product is otherwise identical, and it depends on what the buyer believes
about how it was made rather than on anything they can see.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://theconversation.com/does-made-with-love-sell-research-reveals-who-values-handmade-products-the-most-247351 -->

So the same conclusion arrives from three directions, and it is not a sentimental one.

The thing that carries value is the evidence of human involvement — in what you make, and in the life of the
person making it. Generation cannot supply that, not because the machine is bad at producing artefacts, but
because the artefact was never quite what was being sold.

### Not every piece of work carries the same premium

There is a trap in reading all of the above as "everything you make must show your fingerprints". It does
not, and treating every task that way is its own kind of waste.

The research suggests a division, and it is worth sorting your own work into it, because the three cases
call for almost opposite behaviour.

**Commodity output.** Summaries, transcripts, routine reports, standard correspondence, first-pass analysis.
Nobody pays a premium for the fact that you personally wrote these; they pay for them to exist, correctly
and on time. This is where the tool earns its keep, and where trying to make each one a personal statement
is a way to spend an afternoon on something nobody valued. Generate it, check it, send it.

**Reputation-bearing output.** The proposal that wins the work, the design the client picks you for, the
piece that carries your name into a market. Here the process is part of the product, and the premium exists
precisely because people can tell — or believe — that a person was involved. This is where you do not hide
the effort, and where the choices you made are worth showing.

**Personal work.** The thing you make that nobody buys. The wellbeing finding lives here, and the relevant
question is not what it is worth to a buyer but what the making does for you.

The failure modes are all mismatches.

Treat commodity output as personal work and you burn your hours on the part of the job that was never going
to be visible. Treat reputation-bearing work as commodity and you throw away the thing people were paying
for. Treat personal work as an output problem and you get a result — perhaps even a good one — while
skipping the part that was doing something for you.

The test that sorts them is one question, and it is answerable in a few seconds: **who would pay more
because they knew this was mine?**

If the honest answer is nobody, you are in the first category, and the tool should do most of it. If the
answer is a specific set of people — clients, an audience, an employer who chose you — you are in the second,
and the visible process is not optional decoration. If the answer is *me*, you are in the third, and the
arithmetic of the market is beside the point.

## Where to put the boundary in your own work

So what do you actually do, given that the tool is genuinely good and the collective trap is genuine?

The answer is a division of labour that the research implies but does not spell out, and it rests on one
test. **Sort each part of the work into what the generator is good at and what only you can be responsible
for.**

The generator is good at volume and speed. Given a starting point it will produce twenty versions. It will
produce them faster than you can read them. It will apply a style consistently. It has absorbed more
examples of more forms than you will see in a lifetime.

What it cannot do is any of these:

**Decide what the thing is for.** The model optimises against the prompt it is given. The prompt is the
intention, and the intention is the part you have and it does not. Ch. 11 made this point about context; it
is more true about purpose.

**Choose among its own options.** This is where the experiment above bites. If you take the model's five
ideas and pick one, you have narrowed your work to the model's distribution — which is precisely how a set
of individually better stories becomes collectively duller.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11244532/ -->

**Be accountable for the result.** Ch. 03's argument, unchanged: someone has to be the one whose name is on
it and who is answerable if it is wrong.

**Have been there.** The effort and the narrative that people pay for cannot be generated, because they are
facts about you, not about the text.

So the practical rule is not "use less AI". It is narrower and more useful: **use the generator for volume,
and keep the choices for yourself — including the choice of what to generate from.**

The last clause is the one that gets skipped, and it is the answer to the dilemma.

If you accept the model's framing of the question, you are already inside its distribution. The way off the
average is to bring something the model did not have: the thing you noticed that nobody asked about, the
angle from your own history, the constraint from a real situation. Then let the machine do the twenty
drafts of *that*.

A concrete example, because this is easy to nod at and hard to do.

Suppose you write. The dilemma-shaped way to work is to ask for story ideas and pick the best one. The other
way is to write down, in one sentence, the specific thing you keep noticing that you have not seen written —
and then use the model to explore *that*, to argue with it, to generate the versions you would have taken a
week to reach.

Both use the tool heavily. Only one of them keeps you as the source of the slope rather than a point on
someone else's.

There is an honest limit to this advice, and it is the reason the chapter has caveats rather than rules.
Telling you to originate rather than select is easy to write and hard to do under deadline, and most people,
most weeks, will do the easy version. The experiment suggests that is fine individually and costly
collectively, which is exactly the dilemma — and the chapter's job is to name the trade, not to pretend it
can be dissolved.

## The honest caveats

**The dilemma result is one experiment.** One task — short fiction — one model, one sample. The effect on
rated quality is clear within it, and the reduction in diversity is measurable, but extending either to all
creative work is not supported. It is a strong hint about a mechanism, not a law.

**"Perceived effort" is measured, and perception is doing the work in that phrase.** The labelling studies
show that people *believe* effort matters and rate accordingly. They do not show that the effort actually
improves the work. The two can be true at once, and the chapter does not claim the second.

**Most professional work hides its effort, and nobody has tested what that does to the premium.** A painting
carries its brushstrokes. A strategy document does not show the twelve drafts. Whether the human premium
survives in work where the process is invisible is genuinely unknown, and if it does not, a lot of this
chapter's middle section does not apply to you.

**The wellbeing study is correlational and its authors say so first.** People who craft may be people who
were already happier, or healthier, or less pressed for time. The mechanism is plausible and the caveat is
real, and the honest position is that the *direction* is supported and the *causation* is not.

**And the chapter assumes diversity is worth preserving, which is an argument rather than a finding.** The
researchers frame narrowing as a loss, and I have followed them. A reasonable reader could say that more
consistent output of higher average quality is simply better, and that the era of everyone writing their own
idiosyncratic story was not obviously a golden age. I do not have evidence that settles it, and am not going
to pretend the question is closed.

## Do this today

1. **Under 30 minutes.** Take the last creative thing you made with AI help. Write one sentence naming the
   choice you made that the model did not make for you — the angle, the cut, the thing you refused. If you
   cannot write the sentence, that is the finding, and it is worth knowing before you show the work to
   anyone.
2. **This week.** Make one small thing without the tool, end to end, and notice what the process does that
   the output does not. Not as a moral exercise — as a measurement. You are checking whether the wellbeing
   finding applies to you, and whether the effort you can now skip was doing something you will miss.
3. **This quarter.** Pick the part of your work where you are most replaceable and give it a source the
   model cannot have: something from your own history, a real constraint from a real client, an observation
   nobody asked for. Then generate from that, heavily and without guilt. The point is not to use less of the
   tool. It is to make sure you are still the one choosing what it works on.

  — the experiment at the centre of this chapter. Read the discussion, where the social-dilemma framing is
  argued rather than assumed.
- [Bellaiche et al. — *Humans versus AI: whether and why we prefer human-created compared to AI-created artwork*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10319694/)
  — the labelling study, and the cleanest demonstration that effort and narrative are what move the
  judgement.
- [Keyes et al. — arts and crafts and subjective wellbeing (via EurekAlert)](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1052289)
  — the finding that makes the making matter independently of the product, with the correlational caveat
  stated up front.

  earlier result that AI-assisted output tends to converge.
- Ch. 15 of this book, *Judging AI Tools for Yourself* — how to test whether a given tool actually helps
  *your* work rather than the average user's.

  is worth when nothing is being generated at all.
