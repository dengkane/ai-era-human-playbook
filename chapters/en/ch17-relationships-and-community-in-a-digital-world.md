# 17. Relationships & Community in a Digital World

A representative survey of 1,000 American adults, run in May 2026, asked people about their social
interactions with AI chatbots. **27% of internet-using adults** said they have them.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://imaginingthedigitalfuture.org/reports-and-publications/the-rise-of-ai-companions/ -->

Among those users, the answers get more interesting than the headline. **31% described their AI chatbot as
a friend.** **59% agreed that AI gives them the support they need.** 39% said AI understands them better
than most people do.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://imaginingthedigitalfuture.org/reports-and-publications/the-rise-of-ai-companions/ -->

And then, in the same survey, 59% said they would still rather have a conversation with friends or family
than with the AI.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://imaginingthedigitalfuture.org/reports-and-publications/the-rise-of-ai-companions/ -->

Both of those things are in the same people's heads at the same time.

Which is why the honest version of this chapter is not "AI is replacing your friends" or "AI is a fine
substitute for them". It is a question about what happens when a machine becomes a plausible participant in
your relationships — and the evidence is stranger and more specific than either of the loud positions.

> **The one thing to take away:** the thing that protects you is not contact in general. It is being
> integrated into a network of people who would notice if you disappeared — and no channel, including a
> good one, is the same as being in the room.

## Why this is a health question, not a taste question

Start with the stakes, because they are higher than the cultural conversation suggests and much better
evidenced.

A meta-analysis pooled **148 studies covering 308,849 people** and asked a simple question: do people with
stronger social relationships live longer?

The answer was a weighted average effect of **OR = 1.50**, meaning a **50% increased likelihood of
survival**. The finding held across age, sex, initial health status, cause of death and follow-up period,
and the authors concluded that the influence of social relationships on mortality risk is comparable with
well-established risk factors.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371/journal.pmed.1000316 -->

One detail in that study matters enormously for this chapter, and it is easy to skim past.

The effect was **strongest for complex measures of social integration** — being embedded in a web of
relationships — with an odds ratio of 1.91 for that category, and **weakest for binary indicators of living
status**, like whether you live alone, at 1.19.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371/journal.pmed.1000316 -->

Read those two numbers next to each other. Living with someone barely moved the outcome. Being *integrated*
— the messy, many-stranded version where various people know you and would notice — moved it a lot.

The protective thing is not proximity. It is being held in a network.

That distinction does most of the work in this chapter, because it is exactly the thing a channel cannot
supply. A conversation, however good, is an event. Integration is a structure, and structures are built out
of repeated, low-stakes, bidirectional obligations — the kind that feel like overhead until the day you need
them.

## What the chatbot evidence actually shows

Now the part people want a verdict on, and where the research deliberately declines to give one.

**First: who uses these things, and what predicts it.**

A study of **1,131 people** who use one such platform, including real chat histories from 237 of them,
found that **smaller social networks predicted using the chatbot primarily for companionship** — and that
companionship use, in turn, predicted **lower well-being**. The association got stronger the more intensive
the use was, and stronger still when the disclosure was more personal.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.12605 -->

Read that carefully, because the causal arrow is not established and the authors do not claim it is.

Two readings fit the same numbers: people with thin networks substitute the bot for people and get worse, or
people who are already struggling use the bot more intensely and disclose more to it. The study cannot tell
you which, and neither can I.

**Second: the most useful experiment run so far.**

Researchers randomised **981 people** into four weeks of chatbot use with different interaction modes and
conversation types, and tracked loneliness, real-world socialising, emotional dependence and problematic
use. The result was double-sided and is worth quoting precisely.

The experimental conditions produced **no significant effects**. Making the voice more engaging, or the
conversations more personal, did not reliably change the outcomes. But the participants who *voluntarily
used it more*, regardless of which condition they were assigned to, showed **consistently worse outcomes**
— more loneliness, more dependence, less real-world socialising. And the people who trusted and felt
socially attracted to the bot were the most likely to become dependent on it.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.17473 -->

That design is what makes it worth trusting, because a randomised trial can separate the thing you changed
from the thing people brought with them.

It changed the bot and nothing happened. It did not change how much people chose to use it, and that
correlated with everything.

Which produces a conclusion that is uncomfortable in both directions, and is the honest one: **the problem
is not that the machine is seductive. It is that heavy use and worse outcomes travel together, and the
assignment did not cause it.** If you are the kind of person who climbs into it, the tool will not stop you,
and it will not be the reason you were climbing.

That is also why the survey numbers at the top of this chapter are not contradictory. You can genuinely get
support from a chatbot and still, asked a different question, prefer your friends.

Those are different measurements of different things, and the 59% who would rather talk to a person is not a
correction of the 59% who felt supported. It is the same person, holding both.

## The thing a channel cannot replace

If the mechanism is substitution — moments spent with a bot are moments not spent with people — then the
question becomes what the difference actually is. And here there is a clean, if unglamorous, answer.

A four-week study collected **9,791 daily questionnaires from 411 people** and compared how different forms
of contact related to mental health.

Face-to-face communication was **far more relevant** than digital communication. Digital text was still
meaningfully associated with mental health — more so, notably, than physical or outdoor activity. And video
calls, the richest digital channel available, were **only negligibly associated** with mental health despite
carrying far more of the cues that a text message lacks.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10191089/ -->

That last result is the surprising one, and it is worth sitting with.

If the value of contact were information — hearing a voice, seeing a face — then video, which carries almost
all of it, should have performed nearly as well as being there. It did not. The gap between in-person and
video is not the bandwidth.

Which points at what the body contributes and a screen does not: presence, timing, the fact that you cannot
leave as easily, that you are occupying the same space and will do so again next week.

Ch. 16 found the same thing from the other side — people pay more for work they believe a person made,
because the evidence of a person is the valuable part. Here the evidence of a person is, literally, the
person being somewhere with you.

There is a version of this that is easy to dismiss as nostalgia, so let it be specific instead.

The thing you get from a weekly standing lunch, or a five-a-side game, or a choir, is not the conversation.
It is the *standing*. It happens again next week whether or not either of you felt like it, which is what
turns contact into integration — the 1.91 odds ratio rather than the 1.19.

Chatbots are extraordinarily good at the conversation and structurally incapable of the standing, because
standing requires that you can be inconvenienced and that someone else can be disappointed in you.

And the reciprocity runs both ways, which is the part that gets lost. Being the person someone can rely on
is doing something for you, not just for them. A network is not a service you consume; it is a set of
obligations you carry, and the carrying is the benefit.

## The half of the title this chapter has been ignoring

Everything so far has been about relationships — you, and specific other people.

The chapter's title also promises community, and community is a different object that gets quietly collapsed
into "having friends". It is worth pulling apart, because the collapse is where a lot of the practical
advice goes wrong.

A relationship is dyadic and chosen. A community is neither. It has three properties that no friendship
reliably has:

**You did not select everyone in it.** The other people at the mosque, the five-a-side league, the parents
at the school gate — a community includes people you would not have picked, which is precisely what makes it
different from your friend group. A friend group is a set of tastes that agreed. A community is a place that
agreed to be a place.

**It has continuity independent of any member.** The choir continues when you leave and existed before you
arrived. That is why it is durable, and it is also why the obligation it imposes is real: your absence is
noticed, and the thing is diminished by it.

**It has a shared practice or place, not just shared affection.** You do something together, regularly, and
the doing is not primarily about the relationship. That is the whole trick. When the point is the thing, the
relationship accumulates as a by-product — which is the opposite of a friendship that has to be maintained
by intention and eventually runs out of things to say.

Now put those next to the pyramid of the mortality data. The strongest protective effect was not having
close friends; it was integration, the complex measure of being embedded.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371/journal.pmed.1000316 -->

The thing this chapter's title is about is not the friend you call when things go wrong. It is the structure
that would produce a dozen people who notice, and community is what produces that structure.

And that is the part of social life that has actually degraded, more than friendship has.

Most people have a few close relationships; what has thinned is the third place — the league, the
congregation, the union branch, the neighbourhood association, the thing that happens on Tuesdays. Those
have been declining for decades, and the digital world replaced them with something that looks similar and
is structurally different: groups of people who share an interest, who never see each other, and whose
membership costs nothing to abandon.

Which is not to say online communities are worthless — a support group for a rare condition can be the only
such group within a thousand miles, and that is a real function. It is to say that the property that does
the protective work, continuity and non-selection and mutual notice, is the property an online group is most
likely to lack.

And it is the property an AI companion cannot supply at all. Not because the machine is cold, and not
because the conversation is worse. Because a community requires that the other participants have their own
agendas that are not about you — that they can be bored, or preoccupied, or in a bad mood, or disappointed
in you — and a system optimised to serve you has no such thing.

There is no third person in a conversation with a chatbot. There is you, and a reflection, and no one else
whose Tuesday you are part of.

### Where the digital part is genuinely good

Before this becomes an argument for logging off, note that the evidence does not support that reading
either, and the same study that found face-to-face far more important also found that **digital text
communication was meaningfully associated with mental health** — more so than physical or outdoor activity.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10191089/ -->

That is not a small finding, and it matters most for the people this chapter would otherwise leave out.

The friend you can only reach by message because they moved continents. The support group for a condition
affecting two hundred people in the country. The person whose health, or whose caregiving, keeps them
inside. For all of them, the digital channel is not a substitute for a richer alternative — it is the only
version of the thing that exists. Declining it in the name of authenticity would be absurd, and it is not
what the data says.

So the test is not digital versus in-person. It is whether the digital connection is *in addition to*
something, or *instead of* everything. The evidence that heavy chatbot use travels with worse outcomes is
about people for whom it appears to be the latter — and even there, the authors are careful to say the
association depends on what else the user has.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.12605 -->

The failure mode is not the tool. It is having one channel where there used to be several, and no structure
underneath any of them.

## What to actually do

None of the above is an argument for deleting your apps, and the evidence does not support that either —
the chatbot study's own authors note that the association is not uniform, and that it depends on how the
tool is used and what the user's offline situation is.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.12605 -->

The practical question is narrower and more answerable: **is this a supplement or a substitute?**

A supplement is a bot you talk to about a problem you will also talk about with a person. It helps you
think, it is available at an awkward hour, and it does not stand in for anyone.

A substitute is one you talk to *instead* — where the conversation is the relationship, and the
relationship is the only place this is happening.

The tell is not how much you use it. It is what it is displacing, and whether you could say no to it
without losing anything.

Three tests, and they are cheap:

**The disclosure test.** Think about the thing you have told the bot that you have not told a person. If
there is one, you have found the substitution, and it is worth noticing that the bot cannot be disappointed
in you, cannot be hurt by it, and cannot bring it up next week. That is not a feature.

**The standing test.** List the things that happen on a schedule whether or not you feel like it, that
involve other people, and that would continue if you missed one. If the list is short, you are low on
integration rather than low on conversation, and no amount of chatting will fix it.

**The dependency test.** The experiment found that people who trusted the bot most were most likely to
become dependent on it.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.17473 -->

Which means the feeling of "this really understands me" is not evidence that it is good for you. It is the
leading indicator of the thing to watch.

That is the same asymmetry Ch. 13 described from the empathy side: the machine is very good at producing
the signal that makes you feel supported, and the signal is not the same as the support.

None of which requires renouncing anything. It requires keeping the structure, which means maintaining the
boring parts: the standing invitation, the group chat that is mostly logistics, the obligation you would
rather skip.

Those are not the relationship. They are the scaffolding the relationship is built on, and the evidence says
the scaffolding is the part that keeps you alive.

## The honest caveats

**The causal question is genuinely open, and the chapter has not answered it.** The Zhang study cannot
distinguish substitution from self-selection, and the Fang experiment rules out the *bot's behaviour* as the
cause while leaving the *user's behaviour* looking more like a symptom than a cause. Anyone who tells you
the direction is settled, in either direction, is going past the evidence.

**The mortality finding measures integration, not any particular relationship, and not chatbot use.**
Holt-Lunstad's 50% is about being embedded in a human network over years. Whether an AI companion
contributes even a fraction of that is completely untested, and it is the question this chapter would most
like answered. Do not read the meta-analysis as evidence that a bot cannot help; read it as evidence about
what the protective thing has historically been.

**The face-to-face study ran during lockdown.** Stieger et al. sampled people in a specific, strange period
when in-person contact was restricted and video calls were suddenly the main channel. The ordering may look
different outside a pandemic, and this is the finding the chapter leans on most heavily while having the
least confidence in its generality.

**The counterweight was unopenable, and it matters.** A Harvard Business School working paper, *AI
Companions Reduce Loneliness*, reports the opposite direction of effect. Its PDF would not extract as text,
so it is recorded in the notes as rejected rather than summarised here. A chapter that cites only the
studies pointing one way is doing exactly what this book warns about, so it is flagged as the first thing a
revision should open.

**And the strongest social-media evidence was left out on purpose.** The literature linking social media to
mental health is contested, with a standing methodological critique of the experimental studies, and
resolving it is a different chapter's job. Nothing here rests on it.

## Do this today

1. **Under 30 minutes.** Write down the last three things you talked to an AI about that you have not
   talked to a person about. Then pick one and tell someone. You are not confessing anything — you are
   moving a conversation from a channel that cannot act on it to one that can.
2. **This week.** Name the standing thing you let lapse. Not a friendship — a *schedule*: the regular
   lunch, the group that meets, the call that used to happen monthly. Re-establish one, and do it in the
   form that requires you to be in the room.
3. **This quarter.** Build one piece of integration deliberately, where integration means other people
   would notice if you stopped showing up. Join the thing, take the duty, volunteer for the role with the
   small recurring obligation. It will feel like overhead. Per the odds ratios, it is the difference
   between 1.19 and 1.91.

## Further reading

- [Holt-Lunstad, Smith & Layton — *Social Relationships and Mortality Risk*](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371/journal.pmed.1000316)
  — the meta-analysis, open access. Read the section comparing types of social measurement; the
  integration-versus-living-alone gap is the most useful number in it.
- [Fang et al. — *How AI and Human Behaviors Shape Psychosocial Effects of Extended Chatbot Use*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.17473)
  — the randomised trial. The null result on experimental conditions is as interesting as the finding on
  voluntary use.
- [Elon University — *The Rise of AI Companions*](https://imaginingthedigitalfuture.org/reports-and-publications/the-rise-of-ai-companions/)
  — the survey, with the full topline. Worth reading past the headline rate for the internal
  contradictions.
- Ch. 13 of this book, *Meta-Skills for the AI Era* — why a machine can produce the signal of care without
  being answerable for anything, which is the same argument seen from empathy rather than community.
- Ch. 16 of this book, *Creativity When AI Can Generate Everything* — the parallel finding that people pay
  for the evidence of a person, not for the artefact.
- Ch. 19 of this book, *The Last Fortress of Being Human* — what the body contributes that no channel
  carries, which is where this chapter's video-call result leads.
