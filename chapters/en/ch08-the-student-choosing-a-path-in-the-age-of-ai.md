# 08. The Student — Choosing a Path in the Age of AI

In the spring of 2026, two enrolment numbers moved in opposite directions at the same time. At
four-year institutions, enrolment in computer and information sciences fell **8.4%** in a single term.
Enrolment in health professions rose between **6.0% and 7.1%** — its third consecutive year of gains.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://nscresearchcenter.org/enrollment-insights/ -->

That is millions of people making the same decision at once, which is either a correction or a panic,
depending on who you ask. It is worth being precise about what those students are reacting to, because
whatever it is, it is already visible in the market they are about to enter.

Here is the shape of it. Employment of 22-to-25-year-olds in the most AI-exposed occupations now sits
about **19% below** where it would be if it had tracked the same age group in less-exposed work.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/publication/canaries-in-the-coal-mine-six-facts-about-the-recent-employment-effects-of-artificial-intelligence/ -->

Same measure, a year earlier: **15%**. So this is not a cliff that appeared one morning. It is a gap
that has been widening steadily while everyone argued about whether it was real.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/news/canariesaug26/ -->

> **The one thing to take away:** the first job did not disappear. It got repriced, and what it now asks
> for is evidence that you can do the work rather than a credential that says you should be allowed to
> try.

## Why this matters now

If you are a student, or the parent of one, this is the worst possible moment to be reading advice
about it, because the two loudest stories are both wrong in usefully opposite ways.

One story says AI has ended the entry-level job. The other says the numbers are fine and this is
another moral panic about new technology. The first story is not supported by the data — entry-level
postings are down **7.5%** year over year, and entry-level roles are still **46%** of all postings on
Indeed, against 14% for senior ones.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://hiringlab.indeed.com/2026/07/23/the-labor-market-is-tilting-toward-seniority/ -->

The second story is not supported either, for a reason that will matter for the rest of this chapter:
the effect is concentrated exactly where you would expect, in one age group and one kind of occupation,
and it is growing. Something is happening. It is just not the thing either headline says.

The rest of this chapter does three jobs. It shows you what actually changed at the bottom of the
career ladder, so you can stop arguing with a story and look at a market. It shows you why the signal
you are being asked to produce has changed, which is the part that will still be true when you
graduate. And it gives you three questions you can ask about any path — a course, a major, an
apprenticeship, a job offer — that do not depend on predicting which tools will exist in four years.

Which is the whole problem with the advice you have probably already received, so let us deal with
that first.

## The bottom rung was repriced, not removed

The advice most students get is a version of "pick something AI can't do." It sounds prudent. It is
actually one of the least useful sentences in this book, and Ch. 03 explains why: it asks you to
predict the exact boundary of a moving technology, at the one moment in your life when you have the
least information, and it gives you no way to check your answer later.

The evidence points somewhere narrower and much more actionable. What has changed is not the existence
of the bottom rung but its price.

Start with the study everyone quotes, because the quoting has been sloppy and the details are better
than the headline. The Stanford Digital Economy Lab paper, using payroll data from ADP covering
millions of workers, finds that young workers in the most AI-exposed occupations have fallen behind
their less-exposed peers by roughly a fifth. In levels: employment for 22-to-25-year-olds in the two
most exposed quintiles *fell about 11%* between November 2022 and June 2026, while the same age group
in the three least-exposed quintiles *grew about 10%*.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/news/canariesaug26/ -->

Two facts in that paragraph are more important than the famous percentage. First, the adjustment is
happening through **reduced hiring rather than increased firing** — no one is being cleared out; the
door is narrowing. Second, the authors are unusually careful about what they have shown. They report
"no evidence of widespread, economy-wide job displacement", note that the gap shrinks when you control
for education, that some of the divergence predates ChatGPT, and that the ADP sample shows a larger gap
than national survey benchmarks do. They call their own results "early, descriptive indicators."

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/publication/canaries-in-the-coal-mine-six-facts-about-the-recent-employment-effects-of-artificial-intelligence/ -->

Take that seriously, because it cuts against the story you have probably heard. It also means the
paper does not answer the question everyone asks it.

That question is *why*. And here the research splits in a way worth knowing about, because you are
going to see both halves quoted by people who have read one of them. Economists at the New York Fed
estimate that remote work can explain **64%** of the increase in unemployment among young college
graduates, and argue explicitly that the timing points to remote work rather than AI: the rise in
youth unemployment *predates* the diffusion of generative AI, and the divergence shows up in
"remotable" occupations like software engineering but not in non-remotable ones like mechanical
engineering. Their mechanism is mentorship — when you are not physically next to someone, feedback
drops off, and it drops off most for the people who need it most.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://libertystreeteconomics.newyorkfed.org/2026/06/remote-work-leaves-younger-workers-sidelined/ -->

You do not have to pick a winner between those two papers to act on them, and you should be suspicious
of anyone who does it confidently. What they agree on is the part that matters for your decision: the
thing that used to happen in the first year of a job — being trained by someone more experienced, on
work that a beginner could safely get wrong — happens less than it used to. Stanford locates the loss
in AI absorbing the codified, teachable parts of the work. The Fed locates it in the physical distance
between a junior and a senior. Both describe a first year that no longer teaches what it taught.

The employer surveys say the same thing from the other side, and one pair of numbers makes it concrete.
**38%** of employers have moved basic data entry and processing off entry-level workers and onto AI,
and **31%** have raised the experience requirements for entry-level roles as a result.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.ziprecruiter-research.org/economic-insights-research/ai-employer-report-2026 -->

Read those two together and you get the actual mechanism, which is not "the job is gone." It is that
the tasks that used to occupy your first six months — the ones that trained you by being small and
low-stakes — now belong to a machine, and what remains has been re-labelled as needing experience. The
rung is still there. It has moved up. The company still hires, and it wants someone who can already do
what the first year used to teach.

That is a much harder problem than a robot taking your job, and it is also a much more solvable one,
because it tells you what to acquire before you get there.

Two more numbers complete the picture. More than a third of entry-level jobs now require AI skills,
according to employers — nearly triple the share who said so six months earlier — and the share of job
descriptions mentioning AI skills went from **10.5% to 16.5%** in the same period.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.naceweb.org/about-us/press/2026/ready-or-reluctant-employer-expectations-for-ai-skills-meet-student-skepticism -->

Note what that measures and what it does not. It is a survey of employers about their own postings, so
treat the exact percentage as soft; different surveys in the same year put the share anywhere from a
third of entry-level roles to three-quarters of employers saying AI skills give a candidate an
advantage or are required outright.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.ziprecruiter-research.org/economic-insights-research/ai-employer-report-2026 -->

The spread is itself the finding. Nobody has a clean measure of what "AI skills" means in a job
description, which is exactly what you would expect four years into a technology that is being asked to
do a different job in every posting. Do not optimise against a number that vague. Notice instead the
direction: the bar rose, and it rose fastest in the last six months you were not looking.

## The credential stopped being the proof

Now the part that will still be true when the tool names have changed, and it starts with an
uncomfortable symmetry.

For most of the last century, a degree worked as a signal for a simple reason: producing a competent
piece of written work was expensive, and outsourcing it was impossible. If you handed in an essay or a
report, the fact that a plausible one existed carrying your name was itself evidence about you. The
credential and the proof of competence were the same object.

Ch. 03 argues that AI is, structurally, a machine for making plausible text cheap. If that is right,
then any signal made out of plausible text has just been devalued — your résumé, your cover letter,
your essay, the credential itself. This is the same mechanism as the moat argument, pointed at you
instead of at the economy, and it explains most of what looks confusing about the current market.

Look at what employers say they are doing, and then at what they actually did. **70%** of employers
report using skills-based hiring, up from 65% the year before, and **81%** use it when writing job
descriptions. GPA as a screening tool fell from **73%** of employers in 2019 to **42%** — a bigger
single change than the AI narrative usually gets credit for.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.naceweb.org/job-market/trends-and-predictions/employer-use-of-skills-based-hiring-practices-grows -->

And here is the part almost nobody quotes. When the Burning Glass Institute and Harvard Business School
looked at what dropping degree requirements actually produced in hiring, the promised opportunity showed
up in **not even 1 in 700 hires**.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.burningglassinstitute.org/research/skills-based-hiring-2024 -->

Do not read that as "the degree still wins, relax." Read it as: companies announced the change, then
kept hiring the way they already hired, because removing a line from a job posting does not give a
recruiter anything to replace it with. The requirement was load-bearing. Nobody built a substitute. So
both things are true at once — the credential's formal power is falling and its practical power has
barely moved — and if you are choosing a path, the interesting question is what a recruiter could use
instead, and whether you are producing it.

You are, meanwhile, entering a market that will not be gentle about the difference. In the second
quarter of 2026, unemployment for recent college graduates was **5.6%** and underemployment — working a
job that does not normally require the degree you just earned — was **42%**.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.newyorkfed.org/research/college-labor-market -->

That 42% is the number to sit with, because it is the honest version of "the credential stopped being the
proof." It does not mean those people are unemployed. It means the market priced them at something other
than what they had been told they were buying.

So what do you produce instead? The same thing Ch. 03 says the moat is made of, and it is worth being
specific, because "build a portfolio" has become a meaningless slogan.

Produce work that a stranger can check. That means output with a result attached — a repository that
runs, a dataset someone can re-analyse, a shipped thing with users, a design that survived a client's
objections, a repair you did on a physical object that either works or does not. Not a project
description. Not a certificate. The distinction is exactly the one that separates a claim from a
verification, and it is the same distinction the whole book runs on: when the production of confident
text became free, the scarce thing became the ability to tell good output from bad, and to be the
person whose judgment on that is trusted.

The employer data agrees, which is the least obvious fact in this chapter. The skill employers say rose
most in importance over the past year is not prompt engineering. It is **critical thinking**, at 65% of
employers, above workflow automation and data analysis at 60%.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.ziprecruiter-research.org/economic-insights-research/ai-employer-report-2026 -->

Be careful with that finding — it is self-reported, and "we value critical thinking" has been in
recruiting brochures since recruiting brochures were invented. But it lines up with everything else
here: the market is paying a premium for judgment precisely because the cheap text it now has to sift
through makes judgment harder to substitute and easier to fake.

Which brings us to the thing this chapter has been circling. You are being asked to choose a path at
the exact moment when the standard signals are decaying and the replacement has not been built. Every
piece of advice that consists of naming a safe field is a bet on a specific technology boundary at a
date you cannot see. What you can do instead is ask questions that stay answerable after the answer
changes.

## Three questions that outlive the tooling

Here is the framework, and the point of it is that you can run it yourself, this week, on any path you
are considering, without reading another study. Each question comes from a mechanism in the evidence
above, and each one has a tell — a specific thing you will notice if the answer is bad.

### Question 1: Can I verify the output myself?

*Why it matters:* Ch. 03's argument is that verification, not generation, is where value ends up. An
occupation where the work can be checked cheaply by a machine is in a different position from one where
a human has to sign off, and the difference is not about how "creative" the work sounds. Radiology
sounds like judgment; a lot of it is pattern-matching against a known standard. Plumbing sounds like
manual labour; a leak either stops or it does not.

*How to ask it:* find the moment in this path where someone decides the work is good. Then ask: could a
model, given the same inputs, have decided that? If yes, that step is not your job — it is your tool's
job, and you should be the person who owns the tool.

*The tell:* you can describe what "correct" looks like without appealing to anyone's opinion. If the
only way to know whether the output is good is that a senior person says so, you have found a job that
is durable for exactly as long as senior people are expensive.

### Question 2: Does the first year teach me something written down nowhere?

*Why it matters:* this is the mechanism Stanford names directly. They find employment falling for young
workers in occupations that lean on **codified** knowledge — the formal, documented, teachable kind —
and rising for experienced workers in occupations that lean on **tacit** knowledge, the kind acquired
through practice, mentorship, and repeated exposure to real situations.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/news/canariesaug26/ -->

Ch. 05 makes the same split from the Dallas Fed's version of it and reaches the sharper conclusion:
what AI substitutes for is the entry rung — the book-learning end — while it complements whoever holds
the tacit half. If your first year is mostly codified, it is the year most exposed to being absorbed,
and it is also the year you were counting on to teach you the rest.

*How to ask it:* find someone two or three years into this path and ask what they know now that they
could not have learned from a manual. If they cannot answer, you have your answer about the path.

*The tell:* the knowledge lives in people rather than documents. You will notice it as the thing
everyone says "you just have to do it" about — which is not evasion, it is a description of the asset.

### Question 3: Does anyone have to put their name on my work?

*Why it matters:* Ch. 07 argues that what remains sellable in independent work is the part a client
cannot verify and cannot delegate — reputation and accountability. The same trade exists inside
employment. In the ZipRecruiter survey, the entry-level tasks that moved to AI first were the ones
nobody had to sign: data entry and processing. Nobody asks whose entry it was.

*How to ask it:* imagine the work went wrong. Is there a person who is expected to have caught it? If
that person is you, you are accumulating something that does not appear on a transcript and cannot be
taken by a model that has no standing to be held responsible.

*The tell:* the path has a moment where your name is attached to a judgment, and other people act on
it. An internship where you own a decision beats an internship where you assist with one, even if the
second is at a more impressive company.

| Question | A good answer sounds like | A bad answer sounds like |
|---|---|---|
| Can I verify the output? | "I can tell whether this is right, and here is how I'd show you." | "Someone senior has to look at it." |
| Does year one teach tacit knowledge? | "You can only learn this by doing it next to someone." | "It's all in the onboarding docs." |
| Is anyone accountable? | "If this is wrong, it is my call and my problem." | "It gets reviewed by a committee." |

Now the hard part of using a framework like this: knowing when the answer has changed. You are not
going to re-read the Stanford paper every term. So here is the cheap version. The answer is changing
for you if the first year of the path has started being described in terms that used to describe
someone's first week — "you'll get up to speed quickly," "the tools handle most of it," "we'll train you
on the rest." That is the repricing, happening in your specific case, and it is the same sentence
employers used in the survey when they said **31%** of them raised experience requirements for
entry-level jobs.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.ziprecruiter-research.org/economic-insights-research/ai-employer-report-2026 -->

And one more piece of evidence that the crowd is already moving, which cuts both ways. Undergraduate
certificate programmes grew **10.2%** in one spring — the fastest-growing category of all — while
master's enrolment fell **1.3%**.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://nscresearchcenter.org/enrollment-insights/ -->

Shorter, more specific, closer to a job: that is what students are buying. Whether they are right is
genuinely unresolved, and the honest caveats below say why.

## The honest caveats

**The causal claim is not settled, and I have not pretended otherwise.** Stanford says its own results
are descriptive, not causal, and lists the reasons: the gap shrinks when education is controlled for,
some divergence predates generative AI, and their payroll sample shows a bigger effect than national
surveys do. The New York Fed attributes most of the recent rise to remote work. Anyone who tells you
the question is closed is selling something.

**This is a United States dataset.** Almost every number here — ADP payroll, the New York Fed series,
Indeed postings, NACE's employer surveys — describes the US labour market. If you are choosing a path
in another country, the mechanisms may travel and the magnitudes will not. Verify locally.

**The advice costs money and is expensive to reverse.** Telling a student to optimise for tacit
knowledge and verifiable output assumes they can afford a path with a long learning curve and a weak
immediate credential. If you are borrowing to study, a shorter route with a licence at the end is a
defensible choice, and this chapter does not get to overrule it. Debt changes the correct answer.

**Some of the movement you are seeing is a crowd, not a signal.** Computer science enrolment fell 8.4%
and health professions rose for the third straight year. If a large cohort is moving into the same
place at the same time, the entry-level market there re-prices next, and nobody can measure that yet.
Following the crowd out of one field and into another is still following the crowd.

**"AI skills" as a job requirement is close to meaningless right now.** Three credible surveys in the
same year put it at more than a third of entry-level roles, at 74% of employers calling it an advantage
or a requirement, and at 16.5% of job descriptions. Pick whichever suits your argument. That is the
problem.

**And the obvious one.** Ch. 09 is for the person who already has a career and is deciding whether to
change it; this chapter is for someone who has not started. If you are in your thirties reading this
and feeling that none of it applies, that is correct — go one chapter forward.

## Do this today

1. **Under 30 minutes.** Pick one path you are actually considering. For each of the three questions
   above, write one sentence answering it. Keep the sentences. You will want them in eighteen months,
   because the useful question then will be whether they still describe the path or only described the
   path.
2. **This week.** Find one person two to four years into that path — not a recruiter, not a professor,
   someone doing the work — and ask them one question: "What do you know now that you couldn't have
   learned from the documentation?" Their answer, or their pause, tells you whether the tacit half is
   real.
3. **This quarter.** Produce one piece of work with your name on it that a stranger can check without
   talking to you. A working thing, a dataset, a repair, a published analysis with the method shown.
   Then watch what changes in how people treat you, because that is the signal that is replacing the
   credential, and it is worth more than one you can be told you have.

  the reason Question 2 asks about the first year specifically.
