# 02. You're Anxious Because You're Using an Old Map

A map is not the territory. But you navigate with the map you have, and when the terrain moves under
you, the map doesn't get safer — it gets *more* convincing. Every familiar landmark on it becomes a
reason to walk into a ditch.

That is what most AI anxiety actually is. Not a prediction about the future. A collision between a map
drawn for a stable world and a landscape that has stopped being stable.

> **The one thing to take away:** Your anxiety is not evidence that you're doomed. It's evidence that
> your map is out of date — and a map is something you can redraw.

## Why this matters now

Consider the phrase "learn to code." For two decades it was the safest possible advice, repeated by
presidents and CEOs. It mapped cleanly onto reality: software ate the world, engineers were scarce,
and the path from "I learned Python" to "I have a good job" was short and well-lit.

That map was correct for its terrain. It is now producing wrong turns — and the way we try to prove
that is itself an example of the problem.

The proof people reach for is a single number. In 2025, a New York Fed chart put unemployment for
recent computer science graduates at 6.1%, noticeably above the average across majors, and it was
shared everywhere as evidence that the advice had finally broken.

<!-- verified 2026-09-30 — source: https://www.newyorkfed.org/research/college-labor-market -->

The number is real. It is also unusable at the precision people are using it. Those estimates come
from a survey sub-sample small enough that the confidence interval for computer science ran from
roughly 4% to 11% — wide enough to contain both a recession and a boom. On a different measure, the
share of recent computer science graduates holding *any* job was 90% in 2023: down slightly from the
year before, and still above the entire decade leading up to the pandemic.

<!-- verified 2026-09-30 — source: https://agglomerations.eig.org/p/a-viral-chart-on-recent-graduate -->

So the popular figure proves less than it is asked to prove. That does not mean nothing changed. A
better-measured version of the same story came out of Stanford in 2026, using payroll records instead
of a survey: employment for 22-to-25-year-olds in the most AI-exposed occupations fell about 11%
between late 2022 and mid-2026, while the same age group in the least-exposed occupations grew about
10%. The researchers were explicit that this is not economy-wide displacement. It is a gap opening
between two groups of young workers — one that widened from 15% to 19% over the year they have been
tracking it.

<!-- verified 2026-09-30 — source: https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/news/canariesaug26/ -->

Their explanation is the part worth keeping. What declined was work built on *codified* knowledge:
formal, documented, teachable from a textbook or a procedure. What held up was work built on *tacit*
knowledge, the kind that only comes from practice and repeated exposure to real situations. That
claim is not about a job title or an industry. It is about which half of a profession's knowledge is
written down — the same line Ch. 01 drew, one level down.

The people who got hurt most badly were not the ones who ignored the advice. They were the ones who
followed it *precisely*, spent four years and a lot of money, and arrived to find the door moved.

That is the pattern. Not "AI took the job." Something subtler: the map said "start here," the terrain
said the start is now somewhere else, and nothing on the map told you when it changed.

## Anxiety is a signal, not a verdict

Most advice about anxiety is either dismissive ("just stop worrying") or exploitative ("you're right
to panic — buy this course"). Neither is right, because anxiety is doing a job.

Anxiety is not a malfunction. It is a detection system, and it fires when your model of the world
stops predicting what you actually see. The feeling is information: *your map and the terrain have
diverged.*

Two things follow, and they pull in opposite directions.

The first is that the anxiety is not evidence about your future. It is evidence about the *gap* between
what you expected and what you're getting. The gap is real. What the feeling predicts about your life
is not — that part is your map talking, and your map is the thing that's broken.

The second is that it is pointing at something useful, if you let it. "Will AI take my job?" has no
answer you can act on, which is why it produces the same paralysis everywhere you look. *Which
specific belief of mine just stopped working?* has an answer this week. Moving from the first question
to the second is the whole move this chapter is trying to make.

One caution: anxiety is a blunt instrument. It reports "something is wrong" without saying what, so
people aim it at the most visible target available, which right now is AI. What usually broke was not
AI. It was a specific assumption about how your income was supposed to arrive.

## Why old maps are so persuasive

If a stale map were merely useless, you would drop it. The problem is that it is *actively*
convincing, for three reasons that compound.

**It is backed by evidence that was real.** Someone who learned to code in 2015 and got a good job has
direct, personal proof that the map worked. That evidence is not wrong. It's old. Telling "this worked"
apart from "this still works" means noticing a change that happened quietly, and almost nobody does
that unprompted.

**It is socially reinforced.** You heard "learn to code" from people you trusted, and you probably
repeated it to someone else. Recommending an out-of-date map doesn't feel like a mistake — it feels
like giving good advice, because that's what it felt like the last ten times you said it.

**It is confirmed by everyone around you, until suddenly it isn't.** Most people you know are working
from a similar map, so it never gets tested in conversation. By the time the map fails visibly, it has
already been failing for a while — for people you don't know, in places you don't look.

None of this is a character flaw. It is how maps work. The reason to name it is that "everyone I know
says this" is not evidence that a map is current. It's evidence that you're in a group.

## The three maps everyone is still carrying

Most AI anxiety traces back to one of three outdated maps. They were accurate once. Each fails in a
specific, diagnosable way.

### Map 1: The career ladder

*The model:* you start at the bottom, do the tasks, accumulate skill and seniority, and climb. Each
rung is a task you've mastered.

*Why it's failing:* The ladder assumed you build judgement by doing the simpler work first. A 2026
study that broke 923 occupations into individual tasks found AI's exposure concentrating on
cognitive, non-routine work — engineering, IT, finance, law, administration — while face-to-face,
craft and manual work stayed below 10% of work content. The tasks being handed over are not the bottom
of the ladder. They are the practice ground where judgement used to be built.

<!-- verified 2026-09-30 — source: https://www.coface.us/news-economy-and-business-insights/new-study-reveals-which-jobs-are-most-vulnerable-to-ai -->

Take the entry-level analyst. The ladder said: learn the tool, learn the reporting cadence, learn to
build the deck, and in three years you'll be the one running the analysis. The middle rungs — pulling
the data, formatting the slides, drafting the summary — were exactly what the ladder required you to
master first, and they are cognitive work, which is what this wave takes. The top still needs a person who can
tell a defensible story from a merely convincing one. The distance between rung one and that top just
got much longer.

*The tell:* you have a job title whose description is mostly a list.

### Map 2: The skill stack

*The model:* your value is the sum of your skills. Learn more skills, be worth more. Stop learning,
depreciate.

*Why it's failing:* skills are being repriced faster than they can be acquired. A skill takes time to
learn — months or years. A model capability jump takes a release cycle. When the repricing outruns the
learning, "learn more skills" becomes a treadmill where you buy tickets to a race that keeps ending
before you arrive.

Consider a skill with a genuinely long learning curve: financial modelling. Doing it well takes years
— accounting, structure, and the judgment to know which assumptions actually drive the answer. But a
large share of what fills those models — the pulling, the formatting, the standard schedules — is now
generated in seconds. The skill didn't become worthless. It became *narrower*, and the part that got
cut is the part most people spend their first three years learning.

*The tell:* you finished a course in the last year and feel *less* secure, not more.

### Map 3: The credential

*The model:* a degree or certification is a durable signal of capability. Get the signal, get the
access.

*Why it's failing:* credentials encode what was hard to verify at the time they were designed. When
the underlying capability becomes trivially demonstrable, the credential's value drops — not to zero,
but enough to break the plan built on top of it.

The pattern shows up wherever a credential was doing the work of verification. A hiring manager once
needed the degree because there was no cheap way to check whether you could actually write the code.
Now there is a cheap way: put a candidate in front of a real problem with a model available and watch
what they do with it. When verification gets cheap, the signal that existed to substitute for
verification gets cheaper too.

*The tell:* the thing you studied is now something a tool does in a browser tab, and the credential
hasn't changed its price.

## Redrawing the map

A map that survives this era has three properties that the old ones lack. They're not motivational
slogans; they're structural.

**1. It routes around tasks, toward problems.** The old maps all pointed at *doing* things. The new
map points at *deciding* things: which problem is worth solving, which output is actually correct,
which of three plausible answers is the one that won't blow up next quarter. Deciding stays expensive
while doing gets cheap.

**2. It expects the landmarks to move.** Instead of asking "what is true now," the useful map asks
"what is the rate of change." A skill that takes a year to learn and gets repriced every six months is
a bad investment regardless of how good it currently is. The slope matters more than the position.

**3. It has room for consequences you've personally carried.** Not credentials — consequences. You
were accountable when it went wrong. You decided under real uncertainty. You know what this specific
failure feels like in your body. AI can produce the argument; it cannot have had the morning.

| Old map | New map |
|---|---|
| "Learn this" | "Learn to judge this" |
| What's true today | What's changing and how fast |
| Skills as assets | Skills as depreciating inventory |
| Credential as access | Track record as access |
| Do the tasks | Own the outcome |

## How to redraw yours

This is not a one-time exercise. Redrawing the map is the skill now.

**Start with the maps you're actually using.** Write down, in plain sentences, what you currently
believe about how your career works. "I need X to get Y." The beliefs are usually implicit, which is
precisely why they never get checked.

**For each one, ask: when was this true?** Not "is it true." When was it true? A belief about how
careers work that hasn't been re-examined in five years is a belief about a world that no longer
exists. That's not a character flaw — it's how all of us operate by default.

**Then ask the one question that matters: what would have to happen for this to stop being true?**
If you can't name the event, the belief is untestable and you're holding it on faith. If you can name
it, you now have something to watch — and watching is how you stop being surprised.

It helps to see this done once. Here is a composite, assembled from several conversations rather than
describing one person:

*The belief:* "I'm senior in my role because I'm the one who can produce X reliably."

*When it was true:* it was accurate for most of a decade, and it was rewarded.

*What changed:* X became producible by anyone with a subscription. Not as well at first. Well enough,
soon after.

*The falsifier they wrote down:* "If someone junior can ship X to my standard without me, this belief
is dead."

*What they did with it:* stopped defending X and started selling the thing X never covered — knowing
which X to build, and being the person accountable for the call.

Notice what did **not** happen. They did not set out to become the fastest user of the new tool. Tool
fluency was the least durable asset available to them. They moved from producing the artifact to
owning the decision about the artifact, which is a different job with a longer half-life.

## The honest caveats

Five limits worth stating plainly.

**Exposure is not a verdict.** A task being automatable says nothing by itself about whether the job
disappears. David Autor's work on automation found occupations that lose routine tasks and gain expert
ones end up *more* specialised and better paid — proofreading shifted from checking spelling to
helping people write. The same exposure can mean "this role is finished" or "this role is about to
become more valuable", and which one you get depends on what is added around it. The three maps above
describe a direction, not a sentence.

<!-- verified 2026-09-30 — source: https://hai.stanford.edu/news/assessing-the-real-impact-of-automation-on-jobs -->

**Redrawing the map is disorienting, and that's the cost.** A stale map feels safe because it's
familiar. Updating it means giving up the comfort of knowing where you are. The anxiety does not
disappear because you found a better map — it gets *replaced* by the specific, useful fear of
preparing for something real. That's an upgrade, not a cure.

**Some people genuinely can't redraw from where they're standing.** If you're in survival mode — two
jobs, no slack, caring for someone — you do not have the attention budget to re-examine your
assumptions. This isn't a failure of will, and any advice that treats it as one is bad advice.
Redrawing costs attention, and attention is not evenly distributed. Spend it if you have it.

**A new map is not a correct map.** This chapter tells you to date your beliefs and check your
assumptions. It cannot promise the next map will hold. Some people update carefully and still guess
wrong about which way the terrain moves — the future is not obliged to be forecastable. What
redrawing buys you is not correctness. It's a shorter gap between being wrong and noticing it.

**The payoff is slow and mostly invisible.** A stale map feels safe; an updated one feels uncertain,
because you traded a comfortable fiction for a clear view of things you can't control. Nobody gives you
credit for the disaster you avoided by updating early. The benefit shows up as an absence — of a
surprise, of a vanished job, of a skill that turned out to be rentable. Absence is hard to feel, which
is why few people do this and why the ones who do rarely get thanked for it.

## Do this today

1. **Write down three career beliefs** you'd defend in an argument. Plain sentences. No hedging.
2. **Date each one.** When did you learn it? If it's more than three years old, mark it.
3. **Name the falsifier for one of them.** What specific, observable thing happening would prove it
   wrong? Write it down and set a reminder for six months out. That reminder is the beginning of a map
   that updates itself.
