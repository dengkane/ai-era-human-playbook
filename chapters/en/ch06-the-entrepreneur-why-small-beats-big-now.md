# 06. The Entrepreneur — Why Small Beats Big Now

In August 2026, Americans filed 531,728 applications to start a business.

The Census Bureau's own estimate is that about 28,501 of them — roughly one in nineteen — will become
an employer business within a year.

<!-- verified 2026-09-30 — source: https://www.census.gov/econ/bfs/current/index.html -->

Everything in the first number is a decision.

Almost everything in the second is a constraint.

Registering a company has never been cheaper or faster. That part of the story is true, and it is
genuinely new.

But the gap between filing and employing someone is where this chapter lives. It is the gap between "I
could start something" and "the thing I started can carry me."

The AI story has been told as if it closed that gap. In fact it moved one specific cost and left the
others where they were.

> **The one thing to take away:** small teams can now do work that used to require a large one, because
> a specific set of costs moved from fixed to variable — but that is a mechanism you have to use
> deliberately, not a gift that arrives with your registration.

## Scale used to buy one thing: amortisation

Start with why small firms were small before any of this, because the reason is not "they lacked
ambition" and not "they lacked talent."

In 1937, Ronald Coase asked the question economics had mostly skipped: why do firms exist at all, when
the market could in principle coordinate everything through contracts between individuals?

His answer was transaction costs — the expense of searching for a counterparty, negotiating terms, and
enforcing the agreement. Firms exist because doing some things *inside* an organisation is cheaper than
buying them *outside* one. And they grow until the cost of organising one more activity internally
equals the cost of buying it from someone else.

That boundary is not a moral or strategic choice. It is where the two costs cross.

<!-- verified 2026-09-30 — source: https://cmr.berkeley.edu/2025/04/from-coase-to-ai-agents-why-the-economics-of-the-firm-still-matters-in-the-age-of-automation/ -->

Which sounds abstract until you name what the crossing point buys you.

A firm large enough to need a lawyer regularly can hire one. A firm that needs one twice a year pays a
premium for the same work, or goes without it.

The same is true of an accountant, a designer, an engineer, an operations lead. These are not luxuries —
they are the capabilities a business needs to function — and each one carries a fixed cost that only
makes sense spread across enough volume.

So the real advantage of scale was never that big firms were smarter.

It was that they could **amortise fixed costs**: spread a capability across enough transactions that the
per-unit price became tolerable. Distribution, capital, expertise, compliance, credibility, data — every
one of them works the same way. You either buy enough of it to justify the fixed cost, or you go without
the capability.

Small was not a strategy. It was a budget.

That is what changed. Not human nature, not the quality of small-firm ideas. A specific set of things
that used to be fixed costs came apart into per-use costs.

## The part that came undone

The mechanism is unglamorous and worth stating precisely, because the vague version of it ("AI levels
the playing field") is what makes people quit jobs they should have kept.

Compute went first.

Nobody building a product in 2026 buys servers, and the difference between renting and owning is
precisely a fixed-to-variable conversion: you pay for what you use, in units small enough that a
two-person company can start on the same infrastructure a large one uses.

That happened before generative AI, and it is the template for what came next. A category of capability
that used to require capital to acquire became something you rent by the hour.

Then some professional work followed.

A small operation that cannot justify a full-time general counsel still has contracts. A small team
without a designer still has to ship something that looks like a product. These were the classic cases
where the transaction-cost boundary bit hardest: the work was needed, infrequently and in small amounts,
which is exactly the shape that market transactions handle badly.

Generative AI does not replace the professional. It makes the small, infrequent version of the work
something the team can produce itself, which is the same thing as moving that work inside the boundary.

The Census Bureau's data on what firms actually do with the technology is the most useful check on this,
because it is nationally representative and it is not trying to sell anyone a subscription.

In the reference period from November 2025 to January 2026, **18% of firms used AI in a business
function**, or 32% weighted by employment, and adoption was expected to reach 22% within six months.
Among firms that adopted it, the scope was narrow: **57% use AI in three or fewer business functions**,
most often sales and marketing (52%), strategy and business development (45%), and IT (41%).

<!-- verified 2026-09-30 — source: https://www.census.gov/library/working-papers/2026/adrm/CES-WP-26-25.html -->

Two numbers in the same study matter more than the adoption rate. **66% of users rely on AI solely to
augment tasks**, and **AI-related employment decreases occurred in only 2% of firms**.

Read those together and the honest picture of the mechanism appears: the technology is not replacing a
team, it is letting each existing person cover more ground.

For a large firm that means productivity. For a small team it means something structurally more
important, which is that the set of functions one or two people can credibly hold went up.

The same study found diffusion running in both directions — sometimes a firm adopts AI formally and then
workers use it, and sometimes workers use it in their tasks before the firm has adopted anything at all.

That second channel is the one a small business should notice. There is no IT department to approve
nothing, and the person deciding to use the tool is the person doing the work.

Now the part that keeps this honest.

Warin's analysis in *California Management Review* makes the case that AI agents cut transaction costs
*inside* a firm while raising its dependence on the external platform hosting them — that the platform
becomes a new gatekeeper, and an organisation that fragments its work into individually-purchased tools
can end up with lower internal costs and less coherence than it started with.

Coase's framework does not disappear when the tools change. The boundary moves.

<!-- verified 2026-09-30 — source: https://cmr.berkeley.edu/2025/04/from-coase-to-ai-agents-why-the-economics-of-the-firm-still-matters-in-the-age-of-automation/ -->

Which means the correct version of this chapter's argument is a trade, not a win.

You are converting a fixed cost you could not afford into a variable cost you can — and you are paying
for the privilege with a dependency you did not have before. Whether that trade is good depends entirely
on which costs moved and what the platform can do to you later, and neither is knowable at the moment
you sign up.

## Small is not automatically winning

Here is where the data stops cooperating with the story, and this is the most important section in the
chapter.

If the mechanism above were doing what the standard narrative says, small firms should be adopting AI
fastest. The costs that came undone were the ones that hurt them most.

That is not what the survey data shows. Firms with **four or fewer employees are under 20%** AI use —
below the national rate — while firms with **250 or more employees are at 37%**. And over the six months
the Census Bureau measured, adoption **increased among firms with at least 20 employees and did not
change significantly among firms with fewer than 20**.

<!-- verified 2026-09-30 — source: https://www.census.gov/library/stories/2026/05/ai-use-businesses.html -->

The Federal Reserve's read of the same surveys contains the detail that makes this genuinely
interesting, because it shows the small-firm position *getting worse*.

In the earlier survey series, the relationship between firm size and AI adoption was **U-shaped**: the
largest firms and the smallest firms adopted at the highest rates, with the middle left behind. In the
newer series, that pattern has moderated — the smallest cohort now sits **clearly below** the larger size
classes.

<!-- verified 2026-09-30 — source: https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/monitoring-ai-adoption-in-the-u-s-economy-20260403.html -->

And the firms at the very top are not merely ahead; they are in a different regime.

In Information, Professional Services and Finance, very large firms report AI use of **50% to 60%**, or
60% to 70% when weighted by employment. The Census working paper's own summary is that "AI use is
substantially higher in large firms and knowledge-intensive sectors."

<!-- verified 2026-09-30 — source: https://www.census.gov/library/working-papers/2026/adrm/CES-WP-26-25.html -->

So the correct statement is not that small beats big now.

It is that **the option opened and most small firms have not exercised it.** The window and the
walk-through are two different events, and only one of them has happened for the median business with
four employees.

This also gives the necessary boundary around what AI changes. The technology that is spreading is AI
*use*.

AI *production* is one of the most concentrated industries ever measured: the BIS counts **1,246 firms
producing AI across 32 economies**, with **700 in the United States and 250 in China**, and most
economies specialising in only a few layers of the supply chain.

<!-- verified 2026-09-30 — source: https://www.bis.org/publications/working-paper-1343-geography-ai-firms -->

The "small teams can now compete with large ones" claim is true of the businesses that buy AI and false
of the businesses that make it.

Anyone who blurs those two — who tells you that starting a company in the AI era has never been more
open, and then points at an AI lab as the example — has moved the frame without saying so.

The strategic conclusion is therefore narrower and more useful than the slogan.

If your edge is going to come from being small, it will not come from being small *per se*, because the
data says small firms are not converting the new option into an advantage at scale.

It has to come from the specific work that being small does better even when everyone has the same
tools: the narrow relationship that a large firm's account team cannot maintain because it is assigned,
not chosen; the decision you can reverse in a week because you are the person who made it; the
unglamorous market a larger competitor will not enter because the revenue does not clear its internal
hurdle rate.

Those are not AI advantages. They are size advantages that AI has stopped subsidising the alternative
to — and they still require you to pick the right fight.

## The honest caveats

Six, and the first three are the ones that should change your behaviour.

**The whole dataset is American, and the firm-size surveys are self-reported.** Every number here comes
from US government surveys of employer firms. Definitions shifted mid-series — the Census Bureau rewrote
the core AI question in November 2025 to ask about use "in any business function" rather than "in
producing goods or services" — which is why the Fed is comparing a legacy and a new series in the first
place. A definitional change is not a trend, and the moderation in the U-shape could partly be one.

**Nobody has run the comparison this chapter's argument implies.** The claim is that a small team with
AI can now do what used to take a large one. The support is assembled — transaction-cost theory,
productivity effects measured inside big firms, and the cost structure of the tooling layer — rather than
observed directly. I looked for a study that put small teams and large teams on the same task and did not
find one. Treat the mechanism as sound and the magnitude as unproven.

**The direction of the adoption gap is the opposite of what the cheapening story predicts, and nobody
has explained why.** When something gets cheaper, the firms that could not previously afford it should
adopt it fastest. Instead adoption rose among firms over 20 employees and was flat below that.

The Fed reports the pattern without a mechanism. I can offer a guess — procurement, data, and an IT
function that already exists all favour large firms, and a cheaper tool still costs attention to deploy —
but a guess is not a finding, and it is the single question I would most want answered before acting on
this chapter.

**The tooling prices your variable costs depend on are not stable, and the BIS says so explicitly.** The
Bank for International Settlements' 2026 Annual Economic Report notes the scale of AI investment,
describes the circular financing between chip makers, hyperscalers and AI labs, flags the rise of AI and
IT exposure in private credit, and compares the boom to earlier capital-intensive episodes that "ended
with an eventual reversal in investment, inducing economy-wide recessions."

<!-- verified 2026-09-30 — source: https://www.bis.org/publications/aer-2026/progress-peril -->

If you are building on rented capability, your cost base is a bet on a capital cycle you do not control
and cannot see. A business whose model only works while the underlying tool stays at today's price is
carrying a risk it has not priced.

**Platform dependence is real and its price is not knowable in advance.** The source is candid about
this and so am I: the platform that hosts your work becomes a gatekeeper, and the terms you accept today
are not the terms you will be offered once the work lives there. This is not a reason to avoid the tools.
It is a reason not to mistake a rent you pay for an asset you own.

**You will mostly see the survivors.** The chapter's own opening numbers are a reminder: nineteen
applications for every one projected employer business. Advice about how small teams now win is written
by and about the ones that did. The ones that tried the same thing and did not are not publishing. Ch. 12
covers the operating playbook once you are in — offers, pricing, delivery — and Ch. 14 covers the
decision to leave employment, which this chapter deliberately does not make for you.

## Do this today

1. **List the fixed costs you are currently going without, and price them.** Twenty minutes, honestly.
   Write down the capabilities your business needs but does not have — the contract you signed without
   reading closely, the accounting you do badly in a spreadsheet on a Sunday, the design you are faking.
   Then put a number on what the per-use version costs now. The point is not to buy them all: it is to
   see which of your "we can't afford that" constraints have quietly stopped being true, because the
   ones that have are the cheapest growth available to you.
2. **Pick one function to move inside the boundary this week, and write down what you would do if it
   disappeared.** Choose the smallest, most frequent piece of external work you pay for — the routine
   thing you outsource because you could not do it. Move it in-house with AI, then note on paper what
   you would do if that tool doubled in price or changed its terms. You are not doing an exercise in
   pessimism; you are finding out which of your newly-variable costs are load-bearing before a supplier
   finds out for you.
3. **Choose the fight your size lets you win, and abandon one you cannot.** This quarter, name one thing
   your business does better *because* it is small — a relationship a large competitor cannot assign, a
   market it will not enter, a decision you can reverse in a week — and put measurable effort behind it.
   Then name one thing you are doing in competition with firms that have a structural advantage in that
   specific thing, and stop. Section §3's data says being small is not by itself an advantage any more;
   the only defensible version is being small *and* pointed at the right target.
