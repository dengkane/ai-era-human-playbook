# Research notes — Ch. 16: Creativity When AI Can Generate Everything

## Framing note

This is the first chapter of Part IV, and its boundary matters. `chapters/en/README.md` splits it from
Ch. 19 cleanly: **this chapter owns making things *with* the machine in the loop; Ch. 19 owns
experience defined by the machine's absence.** So nothing here argues that you should avoid AI to be
authentic. It argues something harder: that when generation is free, the scarce part of creativity moves
to places the generator cannot reach.

The research supplies an unusually clean version of that argument, and it is a social dilemma rather
than a warning.

**The best experiment on this found that AI both helps and harms, and the two effects are not in
tension — they are the same effect seen from two places.** Writers given AI ideas produced stories rated
more creative, better written, and more enjoyable, with the largest gains among *less* creative writers.
But the AI-assisted stories were **more similar to each other** than stories written by humans alone.
The authors' framing is exactly right: individually better off, collectively narrower.

The second research line explains why the human half still carries value even when the output quality is
comparable. When the *same* AI-made paintings are randomly labelled "human-created" or "AI-created",
people rate the "human" ones higher — and the effect is moderated by **perceived effort** and
**narrativity**. What people are valuing is not the artefact. It is the evidence that a person was there.

The third line is the one that reframes the chapter's title. Making things is associated with wellbeing
— and in one large UK sample, the boost to "life is worthwhile" from arts and crafts was **as large as
the boost from being employed**. That is not about the output at all. It is about what the making does
to the maker.

## Open questions

- **Whether the collective-diversity result holds as models get better.** The experiment used a specific
  LLM in one task. Whether stronger models narrow the distribution further (by being more confidently
  similar) or widen it (by being more varied) is not established.
- **What "perceived effort" means for work where effort is invisible.** The labelling study isolates
  effort as a mechanism, but most knowledge work hides it — nobody sees the twenty drafts. Whether the
  human premium survives that is genuinely unknown, and the chapter says so.
- **Whether the wellbeing effect is from making or from the kind of person who makes.** The study is
  correlational and its authors say so plainly; the chapter reports the mechanism and the caveat
  together.
- **Whether collective homogeneity is actually bad.** The chapter assumes diversity is worth preserving
  because the researchers do. A reader could argue that more similar stories of higher average quality
  is simply better output; the chapter acknowledges this and does not settle it.

## Claims downgraded or dropped

- **"AI makes you more creative."** Downgraded to the measured version: it improved rated quality on one
  writing task, most for the least creative writers, and it made outputs more alike. The unqualified
  claim is not supported.
- **"AI can't really be creative."** Dropped. The experiment measures how outputs are *rated*, and the
  AI-assisted outputs were rated more creative. The chapter does not relitigate the definition.
- **"Human-made is always better."** Softened to "people pay more for it, for reasons that are about
  perceived process rather than quality" — which is a fact about buyers, not about art.
- **"Crafting makes people happier."** Kept but bound tightly to the correlational caveat the authors
  themselves give. It is the best available evidence and it is not causal.
- **Any claim that this chapter tells you how to be more creative.** It does not; it explains where the
  value moved, and the honest version of that is a description, not a technique.
