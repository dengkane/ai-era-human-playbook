# Research notes — Ch. 09: The Mid-Career Switcher — Your Judgment Is the Asset

## Framing note

This is the second of the two chapters written for people at a decision point, and it is the harder of
the two. Ch. 08's reader has no sunk cost. This one has fifteen years of the same thing, a mortgage,
and a much shorter window to recover a bad bet in.

The research found a genuine contradiction at the centre of the topic, and the chapter is built on it
rather than around it:

- The IMF authors argue older workers are, on average, **better positioned** than younger ones, because
  they are concentrated in occupations where AI complements experience rather than substituting for it.
- The Center for Retirement Research finds older workers in AI-exposed jobs have seen **the largest
  relative increase in exits from work** since ChatGPT — and that before ChatGPT, those same jobs
  protected people into *longer* careers.

Both are looking at the same workers. The resolution is the chapter: the exposure is not the problem.
The **cost of re-adjusting** is. Older workers sit in better jobs and are worse at leaving them, and
when the job changes under them rather than disappearing, that combination is what does the damage.

The second thing the research changed: the draft's obvious advice was "retrain." The strongest evidence
available says public training programmes mostly do not produce earnings gains within 30 months. That
finding is from an RCT of 34,000 people, and it does not get to be ignored because it is inconvenient.

## Open questions

- **Nobody separates age from obsolescence.** The CRR brief finds exits rose for 55+ in exposed jobs,
  and says plainly that if R&D cuts hit AI-exposed jobs disproportionately it would exaggerate the
  effect. Some of what looks like an age penalty is a skills-age penalty, and the two are not
  distinguishable in the data.
- **Whether the exits are voluntary.** The CRR brief describes anecdotal reporting of people retiring
  rather than learn the tool, and cannot separate that from being pushed out. The chapter refuses to
  pick, because the practical response differs sharply between them.
- **What a mid-career switcher should actually train in.** The Mathematica result says generic training
  does not pay within 30 months; the sectoral programmes that do show gains are not the same thing, and
  the comparison is not settled anywhere I could open.
- **Whether "AI-complementary" will still describe the same jobs in five years.** The IMF framework
  classifies judges and lawyers as high-complementarity partly on **regulatory** grounds — someone has
  to be responsible. Regulation is a human-side limit, so it can be decided away, which is the test
  Ch. 03 set.

## Claims downgraded or dropped

- **"Mid-career workers are more exposed to AI than young ones."** False, and it was in the draft. The
  CRR brief finds workers near retirement are *just as* exposed as mid-career workers, and the ASA data
  find 49.4% of 50-plus workers in roles insulated from generative AI, above the 42.2% for younger
  workers.
- **"Retrain into tech."** Dropped as the answer. The specific problem is that the earnings evidence for
  generic training is weak and the age-discrimination evidence says the entry is expensive regardless.
  Retraining stays in the chapter, as a thing to sequence rather than a thing to do.
- **"Your experience makes you safe."** Downgraded to "your experience is in the right *kind* of job, and
  that is a different and more fragile thing than safety." The CRR finding is precisely that the jobs
  that protected older workers into longer careers are the ones now showing the largest relative rise
  in exits.
- **"Take a pay cut and start over."** Softened. The chapter argues for a discount on a *transfer* that
  keeps the tacit asset, and says explicitly that a discount on a genuine restart is a different and
  worse trade.
- **Any sectoral-programme placement rate.** The Year Up and Project QUEST results are promising and
  widely cited, and the versions available here are summaries of summaries. Not quoted.
