# Research notes — Ch. 17: Relationships & Community in a Digital World

## Framing note

The boundary for this chapter is set by the index: it owns *the digital mediation of relationships and
community*. It is not Ch. 19 (embodiment, nature, art) and not Ch. 18 (meaning). So the question here is
narrow: what happens to relationships when a machine becomes a plausible participant in them.

The research produced something better than the "AI is making us lonely" essay this chapter could easily
have been. Three findings, and they do not agree with each other in any simple way:

1. **The stakes are medical, not sentimental.** A meta-analysis of 148 studies and 308,849 people found
   that stronger social relationships carried a **50% increased likelihood of survival** — an effect the
   authors compare with established mortality risk factors.

2. **AI companionship is common and the association with well-being is conditional, not uniformly
   negative.** A study of 1,131 users found smaller offline networks predicted using a chatbot primarily
   for companionship, which in turn predicted lower well-being — and that the association was stronger
   among the most intensive and most disclosive users.

3. **A randomised experiment found no significant effect from how the chatbot behaved, but a clear one
   from how much people chose to use it.** In a four-week trial with 981 people, those who voluntarily
   used it more — regardless of which condition they were assigned — showed consistently worse outcomes
   on loneliness, dependence, and real-world socialising.

That third result is the chapter's spine, and it is deliberately uncomfortable for both sides. It is not
evidence that AI harms people. It is evidence that *heavy use and poor outcomes travel together, and
that the assignment did not cause it* — which points at self-selection and at substitution. And the
face-to-face finding closes the loop: in a four-week experience-sampling study, digital communication was
far less relevant to mental health than in-person contact, and video calls — the richest digital
channel — were almost negligibly associated with it.

## Open questions

- **Whether substitution is the mechanism or the symptom.** The Fang experiment shows heavy use and worse
  outcomes travel together without the assignment causing it. That is consistent with AI displacing real
  contact, and equally consistent with lonely people using AI more. The data cannot separate them, and the
  chapter says so rather than picking the flattering reading.
- **Whether AI companionship helps people who have no realistic alternative.** The evidence on
  "vulnerable users do worse" is about averages and it does not rule out large benefits for people whose
  alternatives are much worse. Nobody has run that experiment.
- **What the mortality finding means for a chatbot relationship.** Holt-Lunstad measures social
  integration, not any particular channel. Whether an AI companion counts toward the thing that protects
  survival is unanswered, and it is the question the chapter most wants answered.
- **Whether the video-call result generalises past lockdown.** The Stieger study ran in an unusual period,
  and it is the one finding here that the chapter leans on with a caveat attached.

## Claims downgraded or dropped

- **"AI makes people lonely."** Dropped. The evidence shows correlation, heavy use alongside worse
  outcomes, and no demonstrated causal direction. The chapter says exactly that.
- **"AI companions are good for lonely people."** Also dropped, for the same reason in the other
  direction.
- **"Online community is a substitute for offline community."** Softened to "the evidence on face-to-face
  vs digital runs one way, and the chapter reports it without claiming digital contact is worthless" —
  which the Stieger data does not say either.
- **A specific prescription (a daily minimum of human contact, a digital sabbath).** The chapter keeps the
  spirit and drops the numbers, because the sources measure associations, not doses.
- **"Loneliness is at epidemic levels."** Not used, because the prevalence figures came from poll
  aggregators rather than a source I could open. The mortality risk is the checkable claim.
