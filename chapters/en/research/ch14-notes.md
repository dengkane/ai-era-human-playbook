# Research notes — Ch. 14: From "Employed" to "Self-Employed"

## Framing note

This is the decision chapter. Ch. 06 argued small teams are viable, Ch. 07 described what changes about
the unit of sale, Ch. 12 is the operating manual. This one is about the transition itself: when to jump,
what you are actually giving up, and how to sequence it. It deliberately makes no argument for or
against going independent; it makes the cost structure visible so the reader can run their own numbers.

**The research inverted the chapter's opening.** The cultural script is a leap: give notice, then go
find clients. The best evidence on the question says the staged version is safer — Raffiee and Feng's
study of thousands of Americans found entrepreneurs who left their jobs in stages were **33% less likely
to fail** than those who quit outright to run the business full time. That is a real, large, and
counter-intuitive result, and it is the chapter's spine rather than a footnote.

**The second finding is the one nobody mentions.** Going independent does not just cost you salary. In a
longitudinal study, **25.7% of people who moved to self-employment had no health insurance a year later,
against 8.1% of those who stayed employees** — and they were significantly more likely to report
delaying needed medical care. The KFF benchmark premium data shows what that coverage costs on the open
market. A chapter about the transition that skipped this would be selling something.

The third thing the research corrected: hybrid entrepreneurship is not a waiting room on the way to a
"real" business. It is a destination for many people, and the literature says so explicitly.

## Open questions

- **Whether the 33% is causal or selection.** The chapter flags this directly: people who choose to
  stage their exit may simply be more cautious, better capitalised, or running more viable businesses to
  begin with. The finding is robust across a large sample, but "staging causes success" does not follow.
- **How the calculus changes with remote work and AI.** Both reduce the cost and time to start
  something, which is what the HBR piece credits for making staging easier. If start-up costs keep
  falling, the case for a full leap weakens further — but so does the case for a long, slow hybrid, since
  a smaller business needs less capital.
- **What a sole trader should actually buy instead of employer benefits.** The chapter names the gap
  (insurance, and the time cost of administering everything yourself) and points at the market cost, but
  cannot give a single answer, because it depends on age, state, and income.
- **Whether hybrid is a stable long-run state or a stress point.** Solesvik says some people stay hybrid
  permanently by choice. Whether that is sustainable over decades is not settled in anything I could open.

## Claims downgraded or dropped

- **"Quitting to go all-in is the brave/right path."** Dropped. The strongest evidence points the other
  way, and this is the chapter's opening reversal.
- **"Going independent is basically free."** Dropped. The insurance finding is too large to omit, and it
  is the reason the chapter has a section on costs rather than only on strategy.
- **"You should always start hybrid."** Softened to "staging is safer on average, and the finding is
  about relative failure rates, not a prescription for every situation". Some businesses and some people
  are genuinely better served by a clean break, and the chapter says so.
- **A specific runway number (three months, six months).** Dropped. Unsourced, and the right number
  depends entirely on the reader's costs and how fast their pipeline fills.
- **"AI makes it safe to go independent now."** Softened. The tools lower the cost of starting (which
  the HBR piece also notes), but the chapter keeps this as a mechanism rather than a promise, and Ch. 06
  already showed small firms are not the ones adopting the tools most.
