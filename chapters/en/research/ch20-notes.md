# Research notes — Ch. 20: The Courage to Slow Down

## Framing note

Ch. 19 argued that some experiences lose their value the moment they are mediated. This chapter is about
the thing that stops people having them anyway, which is not technology. It is the reflex to optimise —
the one the rest of the book spent fourteen chapters installing — applied to the hours that were supposed
to be the reward.

The research turned this into a chapter with a hard number at the top and an uncomfortable number in the
middle.

**The hard number: overwork kills, measurably.** In the first global analysis of its kind, WHO and the
ILO estimated that long working hours caused **745,000 deaths** from stroke and heart disease in 2016, a
29% increase since 2000. Working **55 or more hours a week** is associated with a **35% higher risk of
stroke** and a **17% higher risk of dying from heart disease**, against a 35–40 hour week.

**The uncomfortable number: time poverty may be worse for you than unemployment.** The paper that frames
the field — Giurge, Whillans and West in *Nature Human Behaviour* — reports that in an analysis of
**2.5 million Americans, subjective time poverty had a stronger negative effect on well-being than being
unemployed**. That is the single most useful fact in the chapter, because it kills the assumption that the
trade is "comfort now versus security later". It is not; one side of the trade is measurably worse for you
than not having a job.

**And the money question, which is where most of this advice goes wrong, has actually been resolved.**
Kahneman and Deaton's famous $75,000 threshold and Killingsworth's contrary finding were settled in an
adversarial collaboration: the flattening is real but **restricted to the least happy 20%**, whose
happiness plateaus around $100,000. For everyone else, happiness keeps rising with income. The chapter
reports the resolution rather than the folk version, because the folk version ("money stops mattering at
$75k") is used to justify advice the data does not support.

**And one finding that stops the chapter becoming a rest-and-you'll-be-fine essay.** Vacations work, and
the benefits **fade within a week of returning** — in one study, health and well-being among 54 employees
peaked around day eight and were back to baseline inside a week of going back. Which means the answer
cannot be "take more holidays". It has to be structural.

## Open questions

- **Whether time poverty is a cause or a correlate.** The 2.5-million-person finding is striking and it is
  cross-sectional. People who are unhappy may perceive themselves as more time-poor, and the paper is a
  Perspective rather than a causal design. The chapter reports the comparison and does not claim direction.
- **What "slowing down" means when the structural cause is external.** The WHO data implicate working
  hours, which for many readers are set by an employer. The chapter can name the constraint and cannot
  dissolve it.
- **Whether the fade-out is a property of vacations or of returning to the same job.** Both readings fit
  the data, and they imply very different remedies. Nobody has run the study that separates them.
- **Whether the income resolution changes the advice.** If happiness really does keep rising with income
  for most people, then "earn less to have more time" is not obviously right. The chapter says so rather
  than pretending the trade-off is simpler than it is.

## Claims downgraded or dropped

- **"Money doesn't buy happiness above $75,000."** Dropped as the folk version. What the research
  supports is narrower and stranger: the plateau is specific to the least happy 20% of people, and for
  most people income keeps mattering to experienced happiness.
- **"Time poverty is worse than unemployment."** Kept but attributed precisely — a single analysis of 2.5
  million people reported in a Perspective paper — and not treated as causal.
- **"Take more holidays."** Softened into the opposite of the usual advice: the vacation evidence shows
  the benefits fade within a week, so the answer is not more time off but differently structured time.
- **A specific hours threshold to aim for.** The WHO data support 55+ hours as a risk line; they do not
  identify a safe optimum, and the chapter does not invent one.
- **"Slowing down is a mindset."** Dropped. The evidence is about hours, recovery and structure, and a
  mindset chapter would let the reader change nothing and feel they had.
