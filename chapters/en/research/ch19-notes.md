# Research notes — Ch. 19: The Last Fortress of Being Human: Body, Nature, Art

## Framing note

The index draws this boundary deliberately and it matters: **Ch. 16 owns making things *with* the machine
in the loop; this chapter owns experience defined by the machine's absence.** So nothing here is an
argument that AI is bad. It is an argument about a specific class of experience where *mediation itself*
is the loss — where the point is not the output and therefore cannot be improved, delegated, or
experienced second-hand.

The research made that distinction concrete rather than romantic, and one study makes it almost
lab-like.

**Nature has effects that are large, replicated and measured by bodies rather than by questionnaires.**
The WHO European Centre for Environment and Health commissioned two systematic reviews on green and blue
spaces and mental health, and the overall relationship is positive — with the honest caveat, stated in
WHO's own summary, that no single space type is a "gold standard" that works for everyone.

**The cleanest demonstration is the outdoor-versus-indoor comparison.** Participants walked for 15
minutes inside, and on another occasion outside. After the outdoor walk, performance improved and the
P300 — a neural response associated with attention and working memory — showed increased amplitude.
After the indoor walk, it did not. The authors' own conclusion is the striking part: the environment may
matter **more than the exercise itself**, at least for a brief walk.

That is the chapter in one result. Same duration, same activity, same body. The variable was the room,
and it changed the brain.

**And the arts behave the same way — as measured effects, not as metaphors.** In a 14-year follow-up of
6,710 people aged 50-plus, those who engaged with the arts every few months or more had a **31% lower
risk of dying**, independent of demographic, socioeconomic, health, behavioural and social factors.
And a 2026 study of 3,556 adults found that weekly arts engagement was associated with a **4% slower pace
of biological ageing** — comparable to the effect of weekly exercise — with the arts group averaging
about a year younger on one biological-age measure.

Neither of these says "art is nice". They say the activities change the body.

## Open questions

- **Whether these effects are causal.** Almost all of it is observational. The mortality and ageing
  findings are robust to extensive adjustment, and adjustment is not randomisation. The indoor/outdoor
  experiment *is* experimental but tiny (n=30), young (average age 21) and acute (15 minutes).
- **What the mechanism is.** Attention restoration, stress reduction, immune effects, vitamin D,
  inflammation — the WHO summary notes the mixed results on *which* space characteristic matters, which
  suggests several mechanisms rather than one clean one.
- **Whether the arts benefit is the art or the context.** Going to a gallery involves leaving the house,
  walking, and often other people. The studies adjust for physical activity and social factors, and the
  association survives, which is suggestive and not conclusive.
- **What "absence of the machine" should mean in practice.** The chapter can show that mediated versions
  of these experiences are different. It cannot say how much unmediated exposure is enough, and it would
  be inventing to try.

## Claims downgraded or dropped

- **"Nature makes you healthier."** Softened to the measured version: contact with green and blue spaces
  is associated with better mental health across two commissioned systematic reviews, with the WHO's own
  caveat that no single space type is a gold standard.
- **"Exercise outdoors is better than indoors"** — kept, but attributed precisely: a 15-minute walk, 30
  participants, a P300 effect, and the authors' own suggestion that the environment may dominate.
- **"Art keeps you alive."** Downgraded to "frequent arts engagement was associated with a 31% lower
  mortality risk over 14 years, in an observational study whose authors do not claim causation".
- **"The arts age you slower than exercise."** Explicitly not claimed. The UCL data show the effects are
  *comparable* (4% for both on one clock), and on another measure the arts group looked slightly better
  than the exercise group — which is interesting and far too small a difference to rank the two.
- **Any dose recommendation.** Not offered: no study identifies a threshold, and the WHO review says the
  space-type comparisons produced mixed results.
