# Research notes — Ch. 05: The Employee — From Replaceable Part to Indispensable Node

## Framing note

Ch. 04 ended by handing the reader a position. This chapter is the first of the five that turn a
position into advice, and it takes the largest group: people who already work inside an organisation.

The boundary that shaped the research is the one the index sets. Ch. 05 owns *mattering inside an
organisation* and does not touch working for yourself. That ruled out most of the freelance-market
material and the entire "one-person business" literature, which belongs to Ch. 12. It also meant
saying no to several good sources that are about starting something rather than about being useful
where you already are.

Two research findings changed the chapter from the draft I started with:

1. **The rewrite is not a layoff.** The draft opened on "AI is eliminating roles". The Salesforce
   statement does not say that, and the Dallas Fed's youth-employment work explicitly finds the
   adjustment running through reduced *inflow*, not through separations. The chapter now opens on the
   rewrite of job content, which is what both sources actually support.
2. **The people AI helps most are the people it most endangers.** The 34%-for-novices result in
   Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond reads as good news until you pair it with the fact that the training
   value of the entry-level rung is precisely what is being absorbed. That pairing is the spine of
   the chapter, and it only appeared because the two results were read next to each other rather than
   one at a time.

## Open questions

- **The chapter reuses the Dallas Fed's February 2026 piece, which Ch. 04 also cites.** This is
  deliberate and narrow: Ch. 04 uses it for the *experience premium* and the wage-effect split, which
  is a diagnostic about position. This chapter uses only the codified-versus-tacit distinction, as the
  mechanism behind why the entry-level rung is absorbing the change. The two chapters cite the same
  paper for different sentences, but a reader going front to back will meet it twice. Worth revisiting
  if the overlap reads as repetition.
- **Nobody has published how large the "node" effect actually is.** The chapter argues that judgement,
  context and relationships are what survives, and it can support that structurally (they are tacit by
  construction) but not empirically. There is no study measuring the wage or retention premium for
  "being the person others ask". Inventing a number here would be exactly the failure this repo
  documents, so the chapter offers a self-test instead of a statistic.
- **The Salesforce numbers are a single company and a year old.** Benioff's remarks are from September
  2025 and the company's own statement is from the same period. The chapter uses them as an
  illustration of the *shape* of the change, not as a rate. A reader who takes "half the support
  function" as a forecast for their own employer is overreading it, and the caveats say so.
- **METR's February 2026 update weakens its own earlier finding, and the chapter still uses the
  earlier one.** The 19% slowdown is a clean, well-known number from a well-designed study of a
  specific population; the update says that population is now impossible to sample without severe
  selection effects. The chapter uses the *perception gap*, which is the robust part, and states the
  selection problem in the caveats. It does not claim AI slows everyone down.
- **The Gartner entry-level figure reaches the chapter second-hand.** HR Leader names the survey wave
  (Q4 2025), the sample (110 HR heads) and the analyst, which is more provenance than most of the
  coverage gives. It is still a trade-press write-up of a report I could not open, and the chapter
  flags it as a survey rather than a measurement.
- **I did not find good evidence on whether internal mobility actually rose *for the people who need
  it*.** BambooHR shows the share of fills coming from inside rising, which is consistent with both
  "companies invest in existing staff" and "companies have stopped hiring and are stretching the
  people they have". The chapter treats the ambiguity as the finding, which is honest but not
  satisfying.

## Claims downgraded or dropped

- **"40% of jobs in the most-exposed decile have been automated."** Nowhere supported. The observed
  exposure measure in Ch. 04 is a task-coverage measure and I was about to write it as an employment
  figure. Cut.
- **"AI has already eliminated X million roles, per Challenger."** Appeared in several searches
  (a widely repeated "112,713 job cuts attributed to AI in 2026" figure). I could not open Challenger's
  own release, and the aggregators quoting it disagree about whether the number is cumulative, annual
  or year-to-date. Cut rather than cited second-hand.
- **"Gartner predicts half of middle management disappears by 2026."** Reframed. The prediction I could
  verify is narrower and the chapter does not use it; the flattening claim rests on Gallup's measured
  span of control instead.
- **"Experienced developers are 19% slower with AI."** Downgraded to a claim about *self-assessment*.
  The slowdown is real for that sample, but METR's own update makes the population unrepresentative of
  current tooling, so the chapter states the perception gap and does not generalise the direction.
- **A paragraph on "AI-first" memos (Duolingo, Shopify) as a genre.** Would have been the most
  quotable part of the chapter and rests on two well-covered companies out of thousands. Cut: it also
  drifted toward commentary about management fashion rather than evidence about employees.
- **"Everyone is now a manager of AI agents."** An attractive frame with no measurement behind it. The
  chapter's §2 uses what Gallup actually measured — spans widening and individual-contributor load
  rising — which supports the weaker and more defensible claim.
