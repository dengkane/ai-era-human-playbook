# Research notes — Ch. 08: The Student — Choosing a Path in the Age of AI

## Framing note

This chapter is the first of the two written for people who have not started yet. Ch. 05–07 assume an
income that already exists; Ch. 08 and Ch. 09 assume a decision that has not been made.

The research changed the shape of the chapter twice, and both changes are recorded below. The first
draft was going to be "the entry-level job is disappearing." The evidence does not say that. What it
says is narrower and more useful: employment for young workers in the most AI-exposed occupations is
falling *relative to their less-exposed peers*, the adjustment is happening in hiring rather than
firing, and at least one serious paper attributes most of the recent rise in graduate unemployment to
remote work instead. A chapter that picked one of those and ignored the other would be a lot more
quotable and a lot less true.

The second change: the first draft's advice was "choose a major AI cannot do." That is Ch. 03's
argument applied badly, and it is not actionable — no eighteen-year-old can check it, and the list
would be stale before they graduate. The chapter now gives three questions that can be asked of any
path, and a way to tell when the answer has changed.

## Open questions

- **Nobody has a clean number for "how much of the entry-level squeeze is AI."** Stanford's own team
  says so in the revision, and the New York Fed's answer (64%, remote work) is an estimate from a
  back-of-the-envelope calculation, not a causal model. The chapter says this rather than picking a
  side, because a student who bets everything on one explanation is betting on a number that has
  already been revised once (13% → 15% → 19%).
- **Whether the health-professions surge is durable.** Enrolment rose 6–7.1% for a third straight year
  while computer science fell 8.4%. If a large cohort is moving at once, the entry-level market in
  health care will be the next one to reprice, and that is not measurable yet.
- **What happens to the young workers who never got the first job.** Stanford measures employment, not
  careers. The Indeed post cites research that entering a weak market depresses wages for up to a
  decade, but the current cohort is too young for that to show up.
- **Whether "AI skills" in a job description means anything.** Three different surveys measure it three
  different ways (>a third of entry-level roles, 74% calling it an advantage or requirement, 10.5% →
  16.5% of postings). The chapter uses the spread as the point rather than pretending to a single
  number.

## Claims downgraded or dropped

- **"Entry-level hiring has collapsed."** Dropped. Entry-level postings are down 7.5% year over year and
  46% of all postings are still entry-level. Down is not collapsed, and the chapter says so.
- **"AI is causing the drop in young-worker employment."** Downgraded to "AI is one candidate explanation
  and the evidence is descriptive, not causal" — Stanford's own framing, and the New York Fed's remote-work
  finding has to be reported next to it.
- **"Computer science is now a bad bet."** Dropped. What the data supports is that enrolment in it fell
  8.4% in a single spring while the senior-level share of software postings hit 69.3%. Those are two facts
  about a market, not a verdict on a field, and the chapter treats the enrolment drop as a crowd moving
  rather than a signal.
- **"Choose a major AI can't do."** Dropped from the advice. It restates Ch. 03 without giving the reader
  anything checkable, and it is the exact failure mode Ch. 03's own test exists to catch.
- **"A degree no longer pays for itself."** Not supported. Employers are dropping degree *requirements*
  from job descriptions (70%) while hiring barely moved (not even 1 in 700 hires). Those two findings
  point in opposite directions and the chapter keeps both.
