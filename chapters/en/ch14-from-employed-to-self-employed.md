# 14. From "Employed" to "Self-Employed"

The story you have been told about going independent is a single dramatic scene: you hand in your
notice, you walk out, and then you find clients. Whatever happens next is attributed to courage or to
its absence.

That is a bad script, and the best evidence on the question says it is worse than a bad script. It is
backwards.

Researchers at the University of Wisconsin–Madison studied thousands of Americans who started
businesses, and compared those who left their jobs in stages against those who quit outright to run the
venture full time. The staged group was **33% less likely to fail**.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://hbr.org/2014/08/why-going-all-in-on-your-start-up-might-not-be-the-best-idea -->

> **The one thing to take away:** the leap is not the brave version of the transition and the staged
> version is not the timid one. They are two plans with different failure rates, and you get to pick
> using evidence rather than a story about what kind of person you are.

## Staging works, and the reason matters more than the number

Start with the finding, and then with why it holds, because the mechanism is what you can actually use.

The design is straightforward. Take people who start a business, split them by whether they kept their
day job while doing it, and watch what happens. The ones who kept the job and phased it out — the
"hybrid" path — had a materially lower failure rate than the ones who quit first and built afterwards.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://hbr.org/2014/08/why-going-all-in-on-your-start-up-might-not-be-the-best-idea -->

One result from thousands of people is not proof, and there is a specific reason to be careful with it,
which the chapter's caveats spell out. But the direction is consistent with everything else in Part III,
and the mechanism is not mysterious. Three things change when you do not quit.

**You keep the option to be wrong.** A business that fails while you have a salary is an expensive
experiment. The same business failing after you have quit is a different category of event, and it takes
your housing with it. The staged path does not make failure less likely in some abstract sense; it makes
failure survivable, which means you can afford to test more than one idea instead of betting everything
on the first one that seemed good in a bad month.

**You learn before you commit, not after.** This is the part that the "just do it" advice gets
backwards. A person who quits to find out whether clients will pay has chosen the most expensive
possible way to learn the answer. A person who finds three paying clients on evenings and weekends has
learned the same thing for the cost of some sleep.

**You keep the credibility that comes from having a job.** Ch. 09's transfer logic applies here too. The
first paying customers for most independent work come from people who already know what you do, which
usually means people from work. That network is not a reason to stay forever, but it is a reason not to
torch it on day one.

So the case for staging is not caution for its own sake. It is that staging buys information and
optionality at a price you can pay, and the alternative buys neither.

| | Quit first | Stage it |
|---|---|---|
| Cost of finding out you were wrong | Your income | Some evenings |
| What you learn before committing | Nothing | Whether anyone pays |
| Where the first customers come from | People you no longer see | People who already know you |
| If it fails | A gap on the CV and a squeezed runway | A tiring year |

## Two decisions, not one

Here is the reframe that makes the rest of this chapter usable, and it comes from the research on
hybrid founders rather than from the motivational shelf.

Leaving employment is not one decision. It is two, and they are separated by months or years.

The **first decision** is to start doing something on your own account — an evening project, a first
client, a small offer. The **second decision** is to make it your main income. The literature calls
these the first-step and second-step entrepreneurial choices, and it treats them as genuinely distinct
events with different triggers.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://timreview.ca/article/1063 -->

That distinction sounds academic and it is the most practical thing in the chapter. Almost everyone who
struggles with this decision is trying to make both at once. They imagine that starting means quitting,
so they either never start, or they quit to prove they were serious — and both of those are the mistake
the evidence warns about. The first decision is cheap and reversible. The second is expensive and hard
to undo. Making them on the same day is not courage; it is just conflating two questions.

The scale of the first decision is worth knowing, because it is much more common than the culture
suggests. As of spring 2025, there were around **9.95 million unincorporated self-employed workers in the
United States — 5.9% of the workforce**, and that counts only the narrow official definition.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://gigeconomydata.org/research/data-sources/current-population-survey-cps.html -->

So the first decision is not an exotic one. A meaningful share of the people you know have already made
it. The second decision is the one that deserves the ceremony, the planning and the arithmetic in this
chapter, and it is the one most people rush because they have bundled it with the first.

The practical consequence is a rule for the order of operations. **Make the first decision now, and
treat the second as something you earn your way into.** The rest of this chapter is about the second.

### The first step, concretely

Because "start doing something" is exactly the kind of advice that helps nobody, here is what the first
decision actually consists of.

Its goal is not to start a company. It is to find one person who is not your friend and who pays you for
something you have already delivered. Everything else is decoration.

That means most of what people do first is unnecessary. You do not need a company name, a registered
entity, a website, a logo, a business card or a plan document. Every one of those is either free or
trivial to add later, and every one of them is a way to feel like you have started without ever testing
the only question that matters: will somebody pay.

What you do need is a specific deliverable that someone can say yes to. Ch. 12 has the test — a piece of
work with edges, that a stranger could buy without a conversation. Find it in the work you already do.
The reliable place to look is the task everyone in your field complains about, the tedious one that is
nobody's favourite and therefore nobody's business: the review nobody wants to run, the setup everyone
dreads, the write-up that always gets done last.

The time budget is smaller than people expect. Five hours a week is enough, if the five hours are aimed
at one deliverable for one buyer rather than spread across building a brand.

And the finish line for the first step is worth stating precisely, because it is easy to move: **a
stranger, or at least someone who is not a close friend, has paid you money for a piece of work you
delivered.** Until that has happened, you have not made the first decision. You have made a plan, which
is a different and much more comfortable thing.

## What going independent actually costs, and it is not the salary

Now the part that the inspirational version of this chapter would skip, and it is the part that decides
whether any of the above is affordable.

Going independent does not just replace your salary with your revenue. It removes a set of things that
your employer was quietly paying for, and most of them are not obvious until the first bill arrives.

The largest is health insurance, and the evidence on this is unusually clear. A longitudinal study
tracked working-age Americans through the transition and found that **25.7% of the people who moved to
self-employment had no health insurance a year later**, against **8.1% of those who remained employees**.
After adjusting for age, sex, race and the rest, the gap stayed at about 18 percentage points. And it
was not only about paperwork: those who made the move were also significantly more likely to report
**delaying medical care they needed**.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7968939/ -->

That is the number to hold onto when someone describes going independent as "the same income, no boss".
One in four people who did it lost their coverage.

Which raises the immediate question of what coverage costs if you buy it yourself. KFF tracks the
benchmark silver plan, the reference price for individual insurance, and for 2026 the national average
was **$625 a month** — up from **$497** the year before.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.kff.org/affordable-care-act/state-indicator/marketplace-average-benchmark-premiums/ -->

Read those two figures together and the real cost of the transition appears. It is not a one-off
decision to leave a job. It is a recurring monthly bill that your employer used to pay, and it lands
whether or not the business has a good month. Which is exactly the kind of cost that a plan running on
hope tends to discover late.

The other costs are smaller and easier to underestimate:

**The unpaid half of the work.** Ch. 12's arithmetic applies even more sharply in the first year.
Prospecting, invoicing, quoting, the tax return, the website, the insurance comparison — all of it is now
yours, and none of it is billable.

**The loss of the invisible infrastructure.** Someone used to handle your IT, your HR questions, your
contract templates, your payroll. That is now a set of problems you solve personally, and they arrive
at a steady slow rate rather than all at once.

**The end of the thing that stops you working.** A job has edges. It ends at some point in the evening
and it has weekends built in. When you are the whole business, the thing that tells you to stop is only
your own judgment, and that is the same faculty you are already using all day. Ch. 20 is about this
specific problem, and it is not a hypothetical one.

## What your contract has already decided

There is a constraint that decides the shape of your transition before you have made any of the choices
above, and the cheerful version of this chapter usually leaves it out.

The staged path assumes you are allowed to do paid work outside your job. A large number of people are
not, and the reasons are mundane rather than dramatic.

**The contract itself.** Employment agreements routinely contain clauses that reach further than most
people realise: non-compete terms that restrict where you can work afterwards, intellectual-property
clauses that assign anything you build to the employer, and full-time-commitment language that makes
outside commercial work a breach on its own. You may have signed one and forgotten it.

**Rules of your profession.** If you are a lawyer, a doctor, an accountant or in another regulated
trade, the constraint is not only contractual. There are rules about practicing outside a licensed
entity, about who may hold client money, and about conflicts of interest that a personal side business
can trip without anyone intending it.

**The practical boundary, which is the one that matters most.** Even with a permissive contract, the
first customers for independent work are usually the people who know you from your job — which is
precisely the group your employer will regard as theirs. The question is not only what is legal. It is
what you are willing to be seen doing by the people whose reference you may need later.

Check three things before you plan anything: your contract's non-compete and IP clauses, whether your
profession imposes its own rules, and whether your first plausible customers overlap with your
employer's. If all three are clear, the staged path is available and the rest of this chapter applies.
If any of them is not, you are in the situation the caveats describe: your transition is a leap whether
or not you would have preferred otherwise, and the honest response is to plan it as one — with a larger
reserve, a cleaner exit, and the second decision made deliberately instead of by drift.

## The hybrid trap, and the one you have to avoid

Here is where the honest chapter has to disagree with a piece of the optimistic story.

The research does not say that starting part-time is a way station on the road to a "real" business.
It says something more interesting: hybrid entrepreneurs are **not a homogeneous group**. Some start
part-time and intend to go full-time and do. Some stay hybrid deliberately, for years, because the
combination is what they actually want — the stable income and the discretionary project.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://timreview.ca/article/1063 -->

And the population is not small. Around **42% of R&D high-tech start-ups in Germany and 58% in Sweden**
were founded by people who were still employed elsewhere, and hybrids tend to be more educated and
better capitalised than full-time founders.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://timreview.ca/article/1063 -->

So "I'll do it on the side for a while" is a legitimate destination, not just a phase for the timid. But
that does not mean the option is free, and there is one way this goes wrong that is worth naming
precisely.

The trap is not that hybrid is too slow. It is that **hybrid is comfortable in a way that removes the
pressure to decide.** A business that pays a little and costs your evenings can run for years without
ever getting the test that would tell you whether it could be a living. It never fails, so it never
forces anything, and the decision to stop or to commit is indefinitely deferred — which is itself a
choice, made by default.

The way out is not to set a deadline and quit on it blindly. It is to decide, in advance and in writing,
what evidence would move you to the next step. That is the framework in Ch. 10's loop applied to the
biggest decision in the chapter: a prediction, written before the outcome, so that you find out what
actually happened rather than what you hoped.

Concretely, the signals worth naming before you start are:

**A revenue threshold that repeats.** Not one good month — the same figure three months running. A
number that repeats is a business. A number that appears once is a story you will tell at parties.

**A waitlist, or a client saying no to your capacity rather than to your price.** When people want more
of you than you can give, the question has changed from "can this work" to "how do I make it bigger",
and that is the moment the transition becomes a resourcing decision instead of an existential one.

**A clear answer to what you would lose.** This is the one people skip, and it is the one that stops you
staying hybrid by inertia. Write down, now, what you would genuinely miss about employment: the salary is
obvious, but the colleagues, the structure, and the identity are not. If you find that what you would
miss is mostly the salary, the transition is closer than you think. If it is mostly the structure, Ch. 20
is where you deal with that, and you should deal with it before you leave rather than after.

## The honest caveats

**The 33% is a real finding with an obvious alternative explanation, and I am not going to pretend
otherwise.** Choosing to stage your exit is itself a choice that cautious, better-prepared people make.
The staged group may simply have had more capital, more savings, and more patient circumstances than the
group that quit. The study covers a large sample and the direction is consistent, but "staging causes
success" does not follow from it, and the chapter uses it as evidence for a mechanism rather than as a
guarantee.

**The insurance finding is a US result and it is from before the current market.** The 25.7% figure
comes from a study of American workers on coverage data from 2014 to 2017. The ACA and the marketplace
have changed since, and the chapter pairs it with the current premium figure for exactly that reason. In
countries with universal coverage the entire section does not apply, and the loss is your time instead of
your safety net.

**The premium figure is an average for a 40-year-old and it varies enormously.** $625 a month national
average is a reference point, not a quote. Vermont's benchmark was over $1,299 and New Hampshire's was
$401 for the same year, and what you actually pay depends on age, income and whether you qualify for a
subsidy.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.kff.org/affordable-care-act/state-indicator/marketplace-average-benchmark-premiums/ -->

**Hybrid is not always available.** This is the caveat that matters most and gets said least. The
staged path assumes you can do paid work outside your employment without breaching your contract, and
plenty of people cannot — non-compete and IP clauses, professional rules, or simply an employer who
would notice. If you are in that position, this chapter's plan does not apply to you, and the honest
answer is that your transition is a leap whether you like it or not.

**And the chapter is deliberately not making the decision.** Everything here is the cost structure of a
choice, not advice about which way to go. Some businesses genuinely need full-time attention from day
one. Some people are in circumstances where the salary is not worth keeping. Only you know your numbers,
and the whole point of writing the costs down is so that you can run them yourself rather than take a
position from a book.

## Do this today

1. **Under 30 minutes.** Work out what your employer is paying for that does not appear on your payslip:
   health insurance first, then the pension contribution, the training budget, the software, the phone.
   Put a monthly number on it. Most people find it is far larger than they assumed, and that number is
   the true starting cost of going independent.
2. **This week.** Write one sentence that would tell you it is time to leave, in a form that it could
   actually be false. Not "when it feels right" — "when three consecutive months are above X", or "when
   a client I did not approach asks to buy something". Date it. This is the difference between a hybrid
   business you are deciding about and one you are drifting in.
3. **This quarter.** Start the smallest paying version of the work outside your job, if your contract
   permits it. One client, one invoice. You are not building the business this quarter; you are buying
   the information that the leap would have cost you your income to obtain, and you are doing it while
   you still have the thing that makes failure affordable.
