# 05. The Employee — From Replaceable Part to Indispensable Node

Salesforce used to have 9,000 people answering customer support. It now has about 5,000.

Nobody was fired to make that happen. Marc Benioff said so himself, on a podcast: "I was able to
rebalance my headcount on my support. I've reduced it from 9,000 heads to about 5,000, because I need
less heads."

The company's own statement adds the part that matters. It "no longer need[s] to actively backfill
support engineer roles." Several hundred people were moved into professional services, sales and
customer success instead.

Half of the support interactions are now with agents.

<!-- verified 2026-09-30 — source: https://www.foxbusiness.com/economy/salesforce-cuts-4000-jobs-due-ai-ceo-says -->

Read that once more.

Nobody was laid off. Nothing was announced as a crisis. A job simply stopped growing back, and the
people already in it were moved somewhere else.

That is not a layoff. It is also not nothing.

It is the shape this chapter is about — which is why Part II starts with its largest case: the job you
already have.

> **The one thing to take away:** a role that is being rewritten is more common than a role that is
> being deleted, and what gets rewritten first is the part of your work that is written down anywhere.

## The tool helps the newest people most, which is the problem

Start with the most hopeful finding in this literature, because it is also the most alarming one.

The best-known study of AI at work followed 5,179 customer support agents at one company as a
generative AI assistant rolled out in stages. Productivity rose 14% on average, measured as issues
resolved per hour.

The average hides nearly everything. The gain was **34% for novice and low-skilled workers**, and
minimal for experienced, highly skilled ones.

The authors' explanation: the model spreads the best practices of the strongest workers around, letting
new people move down the experience curve faster.

<!-- verified 2026-09-30 — source: https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161 -->

Read alone, that is good news. Read next to what AI is actually good at, it describes a ladder being
rewired.

The Dallas Fed draws the distinction that makes sense of it. *Codified* knowledge is what you can write
in a manual. *Tacit* knowledge is what practice teaches you and nobody can write down.

AI is now very good at the first. So it substitutes for the entry-level rung of a job — the
book-learning end — while complementing the people who hold the tacit half.

<!-- verified 2026-09-30 — source: https://www.dallasfed.org/research/economics/2026/0224 -->

Those two findings are not in tension. They are one finding seen from two ends.

If AI hands a novice the parts of the job that used to be the novice's training, the novice gets
productive sooner and learns the tacit half later, or not at all. Both halves of that sentence are true,
and only one is in the press release.

Which raises an obvious question: can you tell which half you are working on?

Mostly, no. METR ran a randomised controlled trial with 16 experienced open-source developers on 246
real tasks from their own repositories. With AI allowed, they took **19% longer**.

Before the experiment they predicted AI would speed them up by 24%. After living through the slowdown,
they still believed it had sped them up by 20%.

The tool was slowing them down and they could not feel it.

<!-- verified 2026-09-30 — source: https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/ -->

That gap between perception and effect is the most useful number in this chapter, because it cuts both
ways. It should stop you trusting the warm feeling of having been productive, and equally the cold
feeling of being threatened. Neither arrives with a measurement attached.

Context on how far into the workflow this already is, from Gallup: the share of US employees using AI in
their role at least a few times a year went from 21% in 2023 to **40%** in 2025; frequent use rose from
11% to 19%; daily use doubled in a single year, from 4% to 8%.

That is uneven — 27% of white-collar employees use AI frequently, against 9% of production and frontline
workers. Only 16% of employees strongly agree that the AI tools their employer provides are useful for
their work. Meanwhile 15% think AI will eliminate their job within five years, a number unmoved since
2023.

<!-- verified 2026-09-30 — source: https://www.gallup.com/workplace/691643/work-nearly-doubled-two-years.aspx -->

So people are not waiting to be equipped. A survey of more than 1,000 US employees found **59%** using AI
tools their employer has not approved; among those whose employer *does* provide approved tools, 85% have
still used unapproved ones in the past year, and only about a third say the approved tools meet their
needs.

<!-- verified 2026-09-30 — source: https://cybernews.com/ai-news/ai-shadow-use-workplace-survey/ -->

This is worth naming plainly, because it is the first decision this chapter asks you to make differently.

Using AI at work in a way your organisation cannot see means accumulating a capability that lives in your
habits and nowhere in the company's records. That is a personal asset and an organisational risk at once,
and the organisations that handle it well are the ones where somebody says so on purpose.

## The squeeze is coming from the middle of the chart

Now look at the structure around you, because it changes what "doing well at your job" is measured
against.

Gallup measured the average number of people reporting to a single manager: 10.9 in 2024, **12.1 in
2025** — a nearly 50% increase since 2013.

The median has held steady at five to six, so the average is pulled up by a minority of very large teams:
13% of managers now oversee 25 or more people. And 97% of managers report individual-contributor duties
on top of leading, consuming a median of 40% of their time.

Managers whose span widens without their own workload dropping get less engaged, not more.

<!-- verified 2026-09-30 — source: https://www.gallup.com/workplace/700718/span-control-optimal-team-size-managers.aspx -->

Meanwhile the hiring data says organisations have stopped treating external hires as the default.

From 72 million applications, over a million postings and about 6.5 million completed hires, BambooHR
found internal mobility rising from **51% of all fills in 2021 to 62% in 2025**, while the hiring rate
fell from 4.5% to 2.8% and applications per posting roughly doubled, from about 46 to 95.

<!-- verified 2026-09-30 — source: https://www.bamboohr.com/resources/data-at-work/data-stories/state-of-hiring-2026 -->

For anyone under about 25, the door is narrower still, and the mechanism is the counterintuitive part.

Workers aged 22 to 25 in the most AI-exposed occupations have seen employment fall about **13% since
2022**, while less exposed or more experienced workers held steady or grew. The Dallas Fed attributes
this not to layoffs but to a lower *inflow*: fewer people entering those occupations from outside the
labour force.

The 20-to-24 share of employment in the most-exposed occupations slipped from 16.4% in November 2022 to
15.5% in September 2025 — and if all of that decline became unemployment, it would move the aggregate
rate by about 0.1 percentage points.

<!-- verified 2026-09-30 — source: https://www.dallasfed.org/research/economics/2026/0106 -->

Employers will tell you they are doing this. Gartner surveyed 110 heads of HR in the fourth quarter of
2025: **22%** reported that at least one business leader in their organisation had stopped hiring for
some entry-level roles because of AI automation.

And while 95% of organisations had adopted AI in some capacity, only about one in five reported
significant or transformational value from it.

<!-- verified 2026-09-30 — source: https://www.hrleader.com.au/business/28474-ai-shutting-the-door-on-entry-level-jobs-gartner-finds -->

Put those four findings side by side and a coherent picture appears, none of which is announced to anyone.

The layer above you is being stretched thin. The bottom of the ladder is being closed off. The people
already inside must cover more with less support. And the entry point everybody relied on — get in low,
learn the job, become valuable — has had its most teachable component absorbed by a tool.

The practical consequence for someone already employed is a shift in where the opportunity sits.

When organisations fill 62% of roles from inside, the odds that your next role comes from inside your
current organisation go up, not down. The question stops being "how do I get hired elsewhere" and becomes
"who in this building, when something ambiguous goes wrong, thinks of me?"

That is a question you can act on this quarter. Most people never ask it, because they are still
optimising for the door that is closing.

## A node is not the same thing as a part

Here is a way to hold all of the above, and it is deliberately blunt.

Every role has two kinds of content.

There is the part that could be specified: the procedure you follow, the document you process, the
standard reply you send. And the part that cannot: which of several correct-looking answers is actually
correct here, what happened last time someone tried that, who needs to be told before the decision is
announced.

A **part** is replaceable by definition — it is defined by its specification, so anything meeting that
specification can take its place.

A **node** is defined by its connections: it is what other people route through when they do not know
what to do.

The first is what AI is getting good at. The second is what organisations cannot buy in a licence.

The Anthropic Economic Index survey supplies the strangest supporting evidence. Asked to forecast a year
ahead, over **35%** of surveyed Claude users expected AI to be able to do *most* of their work — and the
people who use it in the most automated way were the *most* optimistic about what that means for their
pay, job security and sense of meaning. Confidence and exposure travel together, as Ch. 04 established,
and neither measures anything.

<!-- verified 2026-09-30 — source: https://www.anthropic.com/research/economic-index-june-2026-report -->

The useful reading of that is not "these people are deluded."

It is that the people who delegate most are furthest along in discovering what they should keep.
Automating the specification part is not a failure of ambition; it frees the hours you need for the part
with no specification. The failure would be spending those hours on more specification work, because that
is where volume expands to fill every gain in speed.

So the migration has three pieces, and you can audit yourself against them in twenty minutes.

**Judgement you can show your work on.** Not "I have good instincts", which is unfalsifiable, but "I
chose option B over option A, and here is what I saw that made me think A was wrong." Judgement that
cannot be explained is not distinguishable from luck, and nobody else can rely on it.

**Context that exists nowhere in writing.** The reason the process works the way it does. The account
that is always late but never wrong. The number in the dashboard everyone has quietly stopped trusting.
This is the purest form of the tacit knowledge the Dallas Fed points at, and it evaporates when nobody is
in the room long enough to learn it.

**Relationships where you are the route.** Somebody in another team, stuck and without authority to fix
it, asks you rather than opening a ticket. That is the operational definition of a node, and it is built
by being useful once, unprompted, on something that was not your job.

Ch. 04's position test told you whether your job's premium is being paid to experience or its content is
being absorbed. This chapter is the part you control.

Same job, same title, same chart: the difference between the person whose role quietly does not grow back
and the person asked to stay is almost always distributed across those three things, not concentrated in
a performance review.

## The honest caveats

Five, and the second and third are the ones that should worry you.

**Salesforce is one company and this is a year-old example.** Benioff's remarks and the company's
statement are both from September 2025. "Half the support function" describes one firm's decision, not a
rate that predicts your employer. I use it because the *shape* is instructive — no layoffs, no
announcement, a role that stopped being refilled — not because the number generalises.

**The Gartner figure is a survey I could not open.** The 22% reaches me through a trade write-up that
names the survey wave and the sample size, more provenance than most coverage gives. But I never read the
report itself. It is a survey of what HR leaders say is happening, not a measurement of what happened.
Treat the direction as informative and the number as soft.

**Nobody has measured the size of the "node effect".** The chapter claims that judgement, context and
relationships are where the durable value sits. That claim is structural — those things are tacit by
construction, so they are the last thing a model can absorb — but no study shows that people with more of
them earn more or keep their jobs longer. What exists is about occupations (Ch. 04's ground) or tool
productivity (§1 above). There is no published figure for what this chapter actually recommends, and
anyone selling you one is inventing it.

**METR has since weakened its own result.** In February 2026 METR published an update saying the
experiment design no longer produces a reliable signal: developers most optimistic about AI now decline
to take part in a study requiring them to work without it, and 30% to 50% of participants admitted
withholding tasks they did not want to do unaided. The chapter uses the early-2025 finding only for the
perception gap, which is the robust part. Do not read it as evidence that AI slows people down in
general.

<!-- verified 2026-09-30 — source: https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/ -->

**This advice does not reach everyone, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest.** If your employer is
closing a whole function rather than redefining it, "become a node" is not a plan; the function's
connections are being unwound regardless of who holds them. And if you are in Ch. 04's Position 2 —
qualified, applying, meeting a door that is not opening — then building context and relationships assumes
you have an organisation to build them in. Ch. 08 and Ch. 09 are for that situation. This chapter is for
people who already have the room.

## Do this today

1. **Write down the last thing that went wrong and how you knew.** Not what you did — how you knew. If
   your answer is "I've seen this before" or "something felt off", that is your tacit knowledge
   surfacing, and the exercise is turning it into a sentence you could say out loud to a colleague. Do
   it once and you will notice how much of your competence you have never had to articulate — which is
   also how much of it is invisible to the people deciding your future.
2. **Ask one question about your team's undocumented context, and write the answer down.** Pick the
   thing nobody can explain: why the process has that extra step, why one account is handled
   differently, which report everyone ignores. Ask the longest-serving person this week. The point is
   not to file the answer: "I know why, and I wrote it down" is the difference between an anecdote and
   an asset — and asking is how you become the person others bring context to.
3. **Start the handoff of one specified task, and put the reclaimed time somewhere visible this
   quarter.** Choose the most codified thing you do — the recurring report, the standard reply, the
   routine extraction — and get it to where you trust it enough to stop doing it by hand.
   Then decide in advance where those hours go: the messiest unresolved case your team has, or the
   relationship with no documentation. If you do not choose, the volume of specified work expands to
   consume the saving, and next quarter you are faster and no more indispensable than today.
