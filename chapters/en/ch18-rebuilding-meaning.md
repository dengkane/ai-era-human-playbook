# 18. Rebuilding Meaning

A survey of **42,151 people across 36 countries**, run in 2026, asked whether AI would mean more jobs or
fewer. In **34 of the 37 publics surveyed, people expected fewer**.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.pewresearch.org/global/2026/09/17/globally-more-people-expect-ai-to-cause-job-loss-than-growth/ -->

The intensity is worth noting, because it is not the usual vague unease. In Australia, South Korea and
the United States, around **seven in ten adults** said AI would result in job loss over the next twenty
years. In the US, that share rose by **7 percentage points in two years** — and among 18-to-34-year-olds,
the share who said they were more concerned than excited about AI went from **40% to 55%**.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.pewresearch.org/global/2026/09/17/globally-more-people-expect-ai-to-cause-job-loss-than-growth/ -->

That is a great deal of people expecting a great deal of loss. And buried inside it is a question that
does not get asked directly, because it sounds too grand to be answerable: if the job goes — or changes
into something unrecognisable — what happens to the part of your life that was pointed at it?

> **The one thing to take away:** meaning is not a philosophy problem; it is a measured one, and the
> measurement says the substitute works. What has to be replaced is not the specific thing you cared
> about. It is the fact that you had something.

## Meaning is measurable, which is the good news

Start by taking the grand question seriously enough to look at data, because it turns out there is some,
and it is better than the tone of the conversation about it.

Nearly **19,000 adults across 17 advanced economies** were asked an open-ended question: what aspects of
your life do you currently find meaningful, fulfilling or satisfying? The answers were coded, and the
result is a distribution rather than a sermon.

Family is the most frequently mentioned source in almost every country surveyed, at a median of **38%**
— and people mention it, in places like Australia, Greece, New Zealand and the US, at rates of half or
more.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.pewresearch.org/global/2021/11/18/what-makes-life-meaningful-views-from-17-advanced-economies/ -->

The other sources, in the same survey: a spouse or partner is mentioned by a median of just **4%**
(9% in the US), friends and community by up to **28%** (Australia, with about a quarter in the
Netherlands, New Zealand, Sweden and the UK), and pets by **3–4%**.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.pewresearch.org/global/2021/11/18/what-makes-life-meaningful-views-from-17-advanced-economies/ -->

Two things about that are worth noticing before the chapter moves on.

The first is that **work does not dominate the list.** It appears, and it matters, but the picture people
paint of their own lives is not primarily about employment — which is worth holding onto, because a
chapter about rebuilding meaning after work changes would be a much sadder chapter if work had turned out
to be the centre of it.

The second is that the answer is **culturally specific**, and the exceptions are informative. Family was
the top source almost everywhere, but not in South Korea, Spain or Taiwan. A finding that holds in
fourteen of seventeen countries is a finding about a tendency, not a law, and anyone writing about
meaning should say so.

And meaning is not just a nice thing to have. In a study of **over 13,000 Americans aged 50 and up**,
followed for eight years, those with the highest sense of purpose had a mortality risk of **15.2%** —
less than half the **36.5%** of those with the lowest. The association held across race and ethnicity,
and was slightly stronger in women.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.bu.edu/sph/news/articles/2022/sense-of-purpose-in-life-may-be-linked-to-mortality-risk/ -->

So the thing people say they want is measurable, it is distributed in a particular way, and it correlates
with living longer. That does not make it easy, and the next section is about why it is genuinely hard —
but it does mean the conversation can be about evidence rather than vibes.

## What the loss of meaning actually does

Now the experiment, because it is the one that earns the chapter's title, and because its result is
almost strange in how useful it is.

Researchers hired **140 people** for a one-off half-day job: cataloguing decades of business reports for a
research archive. It was a real job with a real, comprehensible purpose.

Then they randomised those workers into three groups and told them different things about it.

One group was reminded that the work had been valuable — the original story. A second group was told the
archive had been dissolved after the fact and their work was now meaningless. A third was told that too,
but with a twist: the work had turned out to serve *another* purpose nobody had mentioned at the time.

Then everyone was invited to answer some tedious survey questions for no extra pay, with the option to
stop whenever they liked. The number of questions they answered was the measure of motivation.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/businessreview/2017/07/19/meaningless-work-threatens-job-performance/ -->

The group told their work had been pointless put in **markedly less effort**. That is a real finding about
how quickly the ground can move under someone — a half-day of work, a week earlier, framed as futile, and
subsequent effort collapses.

And then the part worth reading twice. The alternative-meaning group **recovered completely**. Being told
the work had served a different, previously unmentioned purpose absorbed the entire negative effect.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/businessreview/2017/07/19/meaningless-work-threatens-job-performance/ -->

The authors' own summary is the sentence this chapter is built on: *it did not matter that the original
meaning was lost; what counted was that the work had had a meaning at all.*

The people who designed this study were studying what happens inside a company when a project is
cancelled. But read it as a statement about a life and it says something unexpectedly hopeful, and
unexpectedly mechanical. The thing that has to be replaced is not the specific content of what you cared
about. It is the **having-had** of it — the fact that your effort was pointed somewhere. When that is
restored, even by substitution, the collapse reverses.

Which is why "rebuilding meaning" is a bad name for the task. You do not rebuild the old structure. You
establish that there is one.

## What AI actually threatens, and what it does not

Now the part that connects all of this to the thing the book is about, and it requires care, because "AI
will take your job and therefore your meaning" is a sentence with two clauses and only one of them is
evidenced.

When work changes under someone, three different things are at risk, and they are not the same size or
the same shape.

**The income.** This gets all the attention, it is genuinely serious, and it is also the most tractable —
because it is a number, it can be planned for, and most of this book is about how. Ch. 09 is about the cost
of switching, Ch. 12 about what to sell, Ch. 14 about the transition. Income loss is dangerous and it is at
least legible.

**The structure.** The rhythm of a week, the place you go, the people you see because you both happen to be
there. This is Ch. 17's ground, and its loss is more insidious than income loss because nobody budgets for
it and it does not appear on any statement. Retired people report it constantly; so do people whose jobs
went remote.

**Being needed.** This is the one that maps onto meaning, and it is the hardest to replace and the least
discussed. Not "I am busy" and not "I am paid" — the specific knowledge that some outcome depends on you,
that if you did not do it, someone or something would be worse off.

The Pew findings suggest the third is doing more work than the other two in most people's accounts of a
meaningful life, and that it is not supplied by employment alone. Family — the top source at a median of
38% — is a place people are needed constantly and are seldom paid for it.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.pewresearch.org/global/2021/11/18/what-makes-life-meaningful-views-from-17-advanced-economies/ -->

Which produces the useful reframe. **If being needed is the mechanism, the risk is not that AI takes your
job. It is that you have concentrated all your being-needed in one place, and that place is the one that
is changing.**

That is a portfolio problem, and it is worth stating plainly because the emotional version of this
argument runs the other way. The instinct on hearing "AI might do your work" is to defend the work — to
insist it cannot be done by a machine, to resist the tool, to hold the ground. But if the ground moves
anyway, you have spent the time defending one asset instead of acquiring a second.

And the alternative-meaning finding is specifically reassuring here, in a way that is easy to miss. The
workers who recovered were not told their work still mattered. They were told it had mattered,
differently, to someone else. The recovery did not require the original thing to be restored.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/businessreview/2017/07/19/meaningless-work-threatens-job-performance/ -->

So a second source of being-needed does not have to compete with the first. It does not have to be what you
do for a living, and it does not have to be impressive. It has to be real enough that someone notices when
you are not there — because that is precisely the property the experiment was manipulating.

### One concrete case, because the abstract version helps nobody

Suppose it happens to you. The thing you are paid for is now largely done by a tool, and your employer
notices before you do.

The income part has an answer, and this book has spent six chapters on it — the work does not vanish, it
gets repriced, and Ch. 16's division of labour applies: the tool produces, you choose and are accountable.
The structure part has an answer too, and it is Ch. 17's: keep the standing things that happen whether or
not you feel like it.

The being-needed part is the one that goes quiet, and it is worth being precise about why, because the
first instinct is wrong. The instinct is that the meaning came from *doing the thing* — from the code, the
report, the analysis — and that if a machine does the doing there is nothing left. But ask what the people
around you actually needed. They rarely needed the artefact. They needed someone who knew what to build,
who could be told when it was wrong, and who could be held to it afterwards.

That is the same structure Ch. 16 found on the creative side and Ch. 03 found at the level of the economy:
the scarce part is not production, it is judgment and accountability. And those are things people need
*someone* for. A tool can write the code; nobody can hold the tool responsible at three in the morning, and
"we shipped it because the model suggested it" is not a sentence any organisation wants to say.

Which means the transition is not "I stop being needed". It is "what I am needed for moves up the chain",
and that is a change in kind rather than in quantity. The risk is not that the need disappears. It is that
you do not make the move — that you keep offering the part a machine now does, and interpret the resulting
irrelevance as being about you.

The Pew numbers say a great many people are expecting this to happen, and expecting it to be bad.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.pewresearch.org/global/2026/09/17/globally-more-people-expect-ai-to-cause-job-loss-than-growth/ -->

This chapter's evidence cannot tell you that it will be good. It can tell you that the collapse people
fear is a collapse of *having had* a purpose, that a repaired one does not have to look like the original,
and that the repair has been shown to work even when it arrives as a single unexpected sentence.

## What the substitute has to have

If a substitute works, the useful question is what makes one work. The research here is thinner than for
the loss, and the honest version of this section says so — but three properties recur across it.

**It has to be true.** The experiment could tell workers a real, verifiable fact about their work. A
manufactured purpose is not a substitute for a purpose; it is a story, and people who said they were
distrustful were the ones who did not recover.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/businessreview/2017/07/19/meaningless-work-threatens-job-performance/ -->

**It has to involve someone else.** This is where the chapter's evidence converges from an unexpected
direction. The largest longitudinal study of adult life there is — the Harvard Study of Adult Development,
tracking men since 1938 and their descendants since — found that **satisfaction with relationships at age
50 predicted health at 80 better than cholesterol levels did**, and that warm relationships predicted
longer life while the isolated died earlier.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2017/04/over-nearly-80-years-harvard-study-has-been-showing-how-to-live-a-healthy-and-happy-life/ -->

The study's director put it in four words: "Loneliness kills." Which is Ch. 17's argument, arriving from
a different door. If the protective thing is being integrated into a network, then the most reliable
substitute for a lost purpose is a purpose that other people are part of, because it does double duty.

**It has to be yours to do.** This is the one the chapter can assert least strongly, because the research
measures meaning rather than inventing it. But the Pew distribution is suggestive: the sources people
name are overwhelmingly things they *are* — a parent, a friend, a member of something — rather than things
they have.

None of this is a formula, and the temptation to write one should be resisted. The evidence supports
"a substitute works" and "true, joint, and yours are the things that show up". It does not support
"here is what your substitute should be", and a book that supplied one would be inventing something at
exactly the moment the reader can least afford it.

### How to find one without waiting for a calling

That said, "the research cannot tell you what to do" is not a licence to say nothing, and there is a
version of this that stops short of prescription while still being useful. The structure below is drawn
from what the evidence does support, and it is deliberately small-scale.

**Do not start with a search for your passion.** The famous advice to find what you love and do that is
not supported by anything in this chapter's sources, and it has the failure mode of producing years of
searching rather than any obligation. The meaning experiment did not ask workers to care about something.
It established that their effort had been pointed somewhere real.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/businessreview/2017/07/19/meaningless-work-threatens-job-performance/ -->

**Start with the obligations you already have and have stopped counting.** This is the one that surprises
people. The Pew data put family at the top of the list almost everywhere, which means most readers already
have the strongest known source of meaning and are treating it as background.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.pewresearch.org/global/2021/11/18/what-makes-life-meaningful-views-from-17-advanced-economies/ -->

Being someone's person is a real answer to the question "is anything depending on me", and the fact that
it does not feel like an achievement is a feature rather than a bug — the experiment's point was that
having-had a purpose matters, not that it was impressive.

**Then test it by absence.** The operational version of "am I needed" is the one Ch. 17 arrived at from a
completely different direction: if you stopped, who would notice? If the answer is a set of people who
would have a worse week, you have a source. If the answer is nobody, that is information, and it is
better to learn it in a quiet month than in a bad year.

**And accept that the first substitute will be small.** The instinct is to look for something
comparable to a career — as large, as structured, as identity-forming. But the experiment's substitute was
a single sentence about a half-day's work, and it fully repaired the collapse.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/businessreview/2017/07/19/meaningless-work-threatens-job-performance/ -->

The reasonable reading is that the substitute does not have to be equal in size, because what is being
restored is a property rather than a quantity. A weekly obligation to six people is not a career, and it
appears to do the same job.

What none of this does is tell you what to care about. That part remains yours, and it is the part that
cannot be researched — which is the honest boundary of what a chapter like this can offer.

## The honest caveats

**The experiment is small, short and artificial.** 140 workers, a half-day task, a survey as the outcome
measure. The authors argue the effect would be *larger* over a real project, which is reasonable and is
inference rather than measurement. Treat the mechanism as demonstrated and the magnitudes as indicative.

**The purpose-mortality finding is observational.** The Shiba study controlled for socioeconomic status,
demographics, baseline health and depression, and still found the gap. But something that makes people
purposeful — health, money, not being depressed — may also make them live longer, and controls reduce that
problem without eliminating it.

**The Harvard study is not a random sample of humanity.** It began with Harvard sophomores in 1938 and
later added Boston inner-city men. That is a narrow pair of cohorts, and the finding has been replicated
elsewhere rather than proven here. It is the best long-run evidence available and it is not a universal
law.

**"Rebuilding" implies a builder, and the research does not tell you how.** The experiment restored
meaning *by fiat* — a researcher told workers a true fact they had not known. In an actual life, nobody
does that for you. This chapter can show that a substitute works and cannot show you how to find one,
which is a real gap and not a modest one.

**And the AI link is a projection, not a measurement.** Nobody has measured whether AI-driven job change
reduces meaning in the way a cancelled project does. The Pew data show what people *expect*; the
meaning-loss experiment shows what happens when work is invalidated. Putting them together is this
chapter's argument, and it is an argument.

## Do this today

1. **Under 30 minutes.** Write the answer to the Pew question about your own life: what is currently
   meaningful, fulfilling or satisfying? Then mark which of those things would survive your job changing
   completely. Most people find the list is longer than they expected, and that they had not looked at it.
2. **This week.** Take one thing on that surviving list and give it an hour it does not currently get.
   The point is not balance. It is testing whether it is load-bearing — you will find out something
   either way, in an hour, for free.
3. **This quarter.** Find or build one "alternative meaning" that passes the three properties: it has to
   be true, someone else has to be involved, and it has to be yours to do rather than yours to have.
   Concretely, that usually means taking on a recurring obligation toward a specific set of people — which
   is also, per Ch. 17, the thing that protects you. That it does both is the closest thing to a free
   lunch this book has found.
