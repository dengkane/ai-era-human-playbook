# 19. The Last Fortress of Being Human: Body, Nature, Art

Two groups of people went for a fifteen-minute walk. One group walked indoors, the other outdoors. Same
duration, same activity, same kind of human body.

Then the researchers measured their brains.

After the outdoor walk, performance on a cognitive task improved and the **P300** — a neural signal
associated with attention and working memory — rose in amplitude. After the indoor walk, **neither
happened**.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9859790/ -->

The authors' conclusion is the sentence this chapter is built on: the environment may matter **more than
the exercise itself**, at least for a short walk.

The only thing that changed was the room.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9859790/ -->

> **The one thing to take away:** for some experiences, the mediation is the loss. You cannot get the
> benefit of a walk by watching one, and there is no version of being in a forest that arrives through a
> screen — not because the screen is bad, but because the thing you are buying was never the picture.

## These are not metaphors

Before the argument, the evidence, because "nature is good for you" is the kind of sentence that sounds
like a greeting card until you look at what was actually measured.

The WHO Regional Office for Europe commissioned two systematic reviews on green and blue spaces and mental
health, and its summary of them is unusually careful. The overall relationship is **positive** across the
literature. But the comparisons between different *types* of green space produced mixed results, and the
summary draws a conclusion worth quoting in substance: there is **no single space type or characteristic
that is a "gold standard" that works for everyone, everywhere and at any time**.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.who.int/europe/publications/i/item/9789289055666 -->

That caveat is what makes the rest of the evidence trustworthy, because it is the kind of thing an
over-claiming field would have left out.

A European scoping review reaches the same broad conclusion — access to greenspace and contact with nature
promote physical activity and are linked to better physical and mental health outcomes — without picking a
winner among the mechanisms.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.ssph-journal.org/journals/public-health-reviews/articles/10.3389/phrs.2024.1606863/full -->

And the arts, which people are even more inclined to treat as a nice-to-have, turn out to behave the same way
when you measure them against a hard outcome.

Researchers followed **6,710 people aged 50 and over** for **14 years**, tracking how often they did
something receptive and cultural — museums, galleries, exhibitions, the theatre, concerts, the opera — and
linking that to mortality records.

People who engaged **once or twice a year** had a **14% lower risk of dying** during the follow-up. People
who engaged **every few months or more** had a **31% lower risk**. The association survived adjustment for
demographic, socioeconomic, health, behavioural and social factors.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7190042/ -->

The authors are explicit that this is observational and that causality cannot be assumed. But notice how
boring the finding is once you accept it: this is not about art making anyone a better person or more
interesting at dinner. It is about a category of activity showing up in mortality records.

That line of work has continued.

A 2026 study of **3,556 UK adults** compared arts and cultural engagement against epigenetic clocks —
chemical marks on DNA that estimate biological age. Engaging weekly was linked to ageing about **4% more
slowly** than engaging rarely, which was the same figure seen for exercising weekly against not exercising.
On a second measure of biological age, the arts group averaged about **a year younger**, against a little
over half a year for the exercise group.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2026/may/engaging-arts-linked-slower-pace-ageing -->

The lead author's framing is the useful one: this is evidence for treating arts engagement as a
**health-promoting behaviour, in a similar way to exercise**.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2026/may/engaging-arts-linked-slower-pace-ageing -->

## Why mediation is the loss

Now the argument, which is not "technology bad" and is narrower than it sounds.

Ch. 16 made the case that creativity survives the machine, because creativity lives in choosing and
accountability rather than in producing. This chapter is about the category where that rescue does not
apply, and the criterion is simple: **is the experience the point, or is the output the point?**

For a great deal of work, the output is the point, and a machine that produces it faster is a pure gain.
Nobody needs to hand-write a summary to have "really" summarised something.

But for the experiences in this chapter, the doing is not a means to an end. It is the end. Which means
anything that stands between you and it is not an efficiency. It is a subtraction.

That is what the walking experiment demonstrates so cleanly, and it is why the result is more interesting
than "go outside more". Both groups got the walk. The exercise happened. What differed was that one group
did it *somewhere*, and the difference showed up in the brain.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9859790/ -->

Three properties separate these experiences from the ones a tool can improve.

**The body is the instrument.** Reading about a forest is not being in a forest, and neither is watching
footage of one. The information can be transmitted perfectly and the experience is still absent, because
what is doing the work is not cognition. The WHO evidence points at a set of mechanisms — stress regulation,
attention restoration, physical activity — that run through the organism rather than through the model of
the world it builds.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.who.int/europe/publications/i/item/9789289055666 -->

**There is no output to optimise.** This is the part that makes these experiences genuinely safe from
displacement rather than merely resistant to it. You cannot make a walk more efficient. There is nothing to
delegate, because there is no product — a fact that reads as a limitation and is in fact the source of the
value. The whole reason these activities do something that a spreadsheet does not is that they have no
instrumental purpose available to be captured.

**The value is statistical, not narrative.** This is the hardest of the three to accept. The arts finding
does not say a particular gallery visit changed a particular life. It says that across 6,710 people over 14
years, the ones who did this regularly died less.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7190042/ -->

That is the opposite of how these experiences are usually sold, and it is why people under-invest in them.

Nobody can show you the counterfactual. You will never know which concert mattered. The effect is real,
measurable, and completely invisible in any given instance — which is exactly the profile of an investment
that a busy person rationally skips.

Put the three together and the "fortress" framing stops being sentimental. These are not the last things
machines cannot do. They are the things that lose their entire value the moment they are mediated, because
they were never about the result.

### The body is not just carrying the mind around

The chapter's title promises body, and so far the argument has gone through nature and art. The body
deserves its own section, because the habit of treating it as transport is the reason people keep expecting
an informational substitute to work.

Consider something you know how to do with your body and cannot explain: ride a bicycle, swim, balance,
throw a ball accurately. You have the concept. You have probably watched it done a thousand times.

And none of that is the thing, which is why nobody has ever learned to ride by reading, and why the tutorial
that teaches it perfectly still leaves you falling over in the driveway.

This is Ch. 05's distinction between codified and tacit knowledge, taken to its limit.

Codified knowledge is the part you can write down — and it is exactly the part AI now handles. Tacit
knowledge is what practice teaches and nobody can write down. The body is where the tacit end is *absolute*:
not merely hard to document, but structurally undocumentable, because the knowledge is stored in a
calibration of muscles and balance that has no textual form.

Which produces a version of Ch. 03's verification argument that this book has not used yet.

For most skills, you can verify an output — the code runs, the report is accurate, the bridge holds. For a
physical skill, **doing it is the verification**, and there is no other kind. You find out whether you can
swim by swimming. There is no artefact that could stand in for that, and therefore no affordance for a tool
to insert itself into.

The body is not a slow input device waiting for a better one.

That has two consequences that matter for the rest of this book.

The first is that physical competence is unusually durable. The other skills in this book are described as
moving targets — recalibrate your frontier, re-test your tools, re-check your context. Riding a bicycle is
not like that. Once it is in the body it stays there for decades, because nothing is competing to replace
the mechanism and nothing about the technology changes what balancing is.

The second is that this is the one domain where being bad at something is the point. In every other chapter,
the goal was competence: better judgment, better pricing, better evaluation. Here, the beginner's
awkwardness *is* the activity.

Which is why the advice to get good at it quickly is a category error, and why the thing that looks like
inefficiency — the falling over, the slow improvement — is just the shape of the experience.

## What this means for how you spend a week

The practical implication is uncomfortable, because it does not take the form of an upgrade.

If Ch. 16's advice is "find where your judgment adds value and do that", this chapter's advice is the
opposite shape: **protect the hours that produce nothing.** Not the hours you spend recovering so you can
work. The hours that are the point.

That reframing has consequences for how these activities get scheduled, and they are worth being blunt
about.

**They do not compete on quality, so they should not compete on efficiency either.** A mediocre walk still
contains the walk. A dull museum has the museum in it. The instinct to make these activities "worth it" —
the right concert, the impressive hike — is a productivity reflex applied where the output does not exist.
The effect measured was regular engagement, not excellent engagement.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2026/may/engaging-arts-linked-slower-pace-ageing -->

**They have to be actually attended, which rules out the optimised version.** The walking result suggests
something specific: what matters is not the calories, since both groups burned them. It is that you were
somewhere.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9859790/ -->

Which means the version where you take the meeting on the walk, or listen to a podcast through the museum,
is not a compromise between two goods. It is the indoor walk with better scenery.

**And the record-keeping instinct is the thing to resist.** Ch. 10 and Ch. 15 both told you to log
everything, because evaluation needs records. Do not do it here. There is nothing to evaluate, and the
attempt to measure the benefit is the same reflex that wants the walk to be efficient. You will not find out
which visit mattered, because the design of the thing does not permit it.

There is one honest tension worth naming. These experiences have a real opportunity cost, measured in exactly
the units the rest of this book spent fourteen chapters helping you earn. A person building a business has an
hour before bed, and the evidence here says that hour is better spent in a gallery than in almost anything
else the chapter could suggest.

That is a genuine conflict, and I do not think it resolves through clever scheduling.

It resolves, if at all, by noticing which side of the trade you are defaulting to. If the hours that produce
something always win, they will win every week, and the evidence is fairly clear about what that costs —
over fourteen years, in records, at a scale large enough to be dull.

### Why this gets harder rather than easier

There is a version of the AI story in which all of this becomes simple. The tools take the producing tasks,
the working week shrinks, and everyone has the time for walks and galleries that they currently do not.

It is a pleasant picture and it has a specific flaw: it assumes the time released comes back available,
rather than being claimed.

The mechanism of the claim is the reflex this book has spent most of its length installing. Ch. 10 told you
to test and measure. Ch. 11 told you to curate every input. Ch. 15 told you to build evaluation sets and
re-test when they saturate.

All of that advice is correct, and all of it trains a habit of asking "what is this for" — which is exactly
the wrong question to bring to a forest.

So the risk is not that AI takes your leisure. It is that a productive person, given more hours, applies the
same optimisation to them. The walk becomes a workout with a target heart rate. The gallery visit becomes
content. The hobby becomes a side project with revenue attached, which is a fate this book has arguably
contributed to by spending Ch. 12 telling people how to productise what they do.

The evidence cannot measure that drift. But it can tell you what the drift costs, because the studies in
this chapter measured the *unoptimised* version. Nobody was told to walk well. The arts finding is about
frequency, not quality — "every few months or more" was the threshold, which is a bar low enough that almost
anyone reading this already clears or could.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7190042/ -->

That is the practical argument for being deliberate here, and it is the opposite of the advice the rest of
the book gives.

Everywhere else, the move was to get sharper: pick better tools, build better tests, choose better prices.
Here the move is to leave one thing unsharpened on purpose, and to notice how strong the pull is to do
otherwise. If you have spent two hundred pages learning to evaluate, the unexamined walk is going to feel
like negligence.

It is not. It is the one category where the evaluation destroys the thing being evaluated.

## The honest caveats

**Almost everything here is observational, and the exceptions are tiny.** The mortality study adjusted for a
long list of confounders and its authors still say causality cannot be assumed. The ageing study is
cross-sectional in its associations. The one genuinely experimental result — indoor versus outdoor walking —
has **30 participants**, an average age of 21, and a 15-minute exposure. Treat the class of finding as
directionally robust and every individual number as provisional.

**The strongest single source could not be opened.** A global analysis of more than **3,800 studies**
covering over **10 million people** found that contact with nature reduces anxiety and depression. It is
exactly the citation a chapter like this wants, and `nature.com` returned a JavaScript challenge to this tool
with no open-access mirror found. It sits in the notes as rejected and is flagged as the first thing a
revision should open.

**The arts effect might not be the art.** Going to a gallery involves leaving the house, walking there, and
often other people. The studies adjust for physical activity and social factors and the association survives,
which is suggestive. It is not the same as knowing that the art is the active ingredient rather than the
outing.

**And the WHO review cuts against tidy advice.** Its own summary says the comparisons between space types
produced mixed results, and that there is no single gold-standard green space. So this chapter cannot tell you
what to do specifically — only that the general category shows up in the data and that blue-space evidence is
too thin to draw on at all.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.who.int/europe/publications/i/item/9789289055666 -->

**Finally, the "fortress" framing has a risk of its own.** It is easy to slide from "these experiences cannot
be delegated" to "these experiences are morally superior", which is a claim nothing here supports. A walk is
a walk. Watching a documentary about a forest in a hospital bed is a genuinely good thing. The evidence says
mediated versions are not the same *as measured by effect sizes* — not that they are worthless, and not that
the people who cannot go outside are doing it wrong.

## Do this today

1. **Under 30 minutes.** Walk for fifteen minutes outside, without your phone playing anything. It is the
   exact protocol from the experiment, and the point is not that you will feel the P300. The point is
   noticing that the same fifteen minutes in the other direction feels like a different activity.
2. **This week.** Pick one cultural thing near you — a gallery, a small gig, a library — and go, at the
   frequency you can actually sustain rather than the one that sounds impressive. The measured effect was
   "every few months or more", which is a much lower bar than most people set for themselves.
3. **This quarter.** Find one recurring slot in your calendar that currently produces nothing and defend it
   against the productivity reflex. Not an efficient version of a leisure activity — the inefficient one.
   If it feels slightly wasteful, you have probably got the category right, because the whole reason it works
   is that it has nothing to optimise.

  same finding reached through a different experiment.

  as a scheduling problem, turns out to be a harder one.
