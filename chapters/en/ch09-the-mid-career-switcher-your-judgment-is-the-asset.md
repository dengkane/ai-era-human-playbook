# 09. The Mid-Career Switcher — Your Judgment Is the Asset

Two papers about older workers and AI, published within three weeks of each other in 2026, reach
opposite-sounding conclusions.

Both are right, and the gap between them is this chapter.

The first, from researchers at the IMF writing for Wharton's Pension Research Council, finds that older
workers are on average **better positioned than younger workers to benefit from AI**.

The reason is where they sit. As people age, and especially if they are educated, they move out of roles
that are easy to replace and into work where experience, judgment and having to answer for a decision
are the point. Independent decision-making and responsibility for other people, the authors note, are
exactly the features that make AI complementary rather than substitutive.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://pensionresearchcouncil.wharton.upenn.edu/blog/artificial-intelligence-and-older-workers-opportunity-risk-and-policy-tradeoffs/ -->

The second, from the Center for Retirement Research at Boston College, finds the same group leaving work
faster than before.

Among workers 55 and over, high AI exposure is associated with a measurable rise in exits from employment
since ChatGPT launched — and unlike every previous wave of automation, the effect is **largest in the
higher-paying jobs**.

Computer programmers: a predicted increase in leaving work of over **25%**. Painters, at the bottom of
the exposure scale: **2%**.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://crr.bc.edu/are-the-careers-of-older-workers-being-cut-short-by-ai/ -->

> **The one thing to take away:** your exposure to AI is not the risk. The cost of changing course when
> you have to is the risk, and that cost is something you can lower in advance and cannot lower in a
> hurry.

## Both papers are right, and the difference is where you are standing

Start with the thing the two findings agree on, because it is not what anyone expects.

Workers near retirement are not less exposed to AI than workers in mid-career. They are *just as* exposed.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://crr.bc.edu/are-the-careers-of-older-workers-being-cut-short-by-ai/ -->

So the "older workers are safer" story cannot be about exposure. It has to be about what happens when the
exposed job changes, and here the two papers are describing two halves of one mechanism.

The IMF framework gives you the vocabulary. Every occupation has two relevant properties.

**Exposure** is how much of the work AI can technically do. **Complementarity** is whether using AI on
that work is likely to make the person more productive or to replace them — and crucially,
complementarity is not a technical property. The authors define it through social preferences and law:
responsibility for other people, independent decision-making, social interaction, physical presence.

A judge and a lawyer have high exposure *and* high complementarity. A telemarketer and a legal assistant
have high exposure and low complementarity.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://pensionresearchcouncil.wharton.upenn.edu/blog/artificial-intelligence-and-older-workers-opportunity-risk-and-policy-tradeoffs/ -->

That distinction is doing a lot of work, and it is worth noticing what it implies. The protection does not
come from the work being hard for AI. It comes from the work being **someone's responsibility**.

Ch. 03 makes the same argument from the other end: what AI cannot absorb is the part where a person has
to be on the hook for an answer, because there is nobody to hold the model accountable.

Now the second paper, which is the one that should worry you.

The Center for Retirement Research tracked what happened to workers aged 55 and older across the ChatGPT
transition, using Current Population Survey panels. It confirms the IMF picture in the baseline: before
generative AI, workers in highly exposed jobs were actually *less* likely to leave employment than
workers in less-exposed ones — **11.7% against 14.1%**.

High-exposure jobs were the good jobs, the ones you could grow old in. Less physical, better paid,
degree-holding.

Then the relationship weakened. Since the launch of ChatGPT, the exposed jobs saw a relative rise in
people leaving work, and specifically in leaving *into unemployment* rather than into retirement. The
advantage that those jobs used to confer on career length has largely been given back.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://crr.bc.edu/are-the-careers-of-older-workers-being-cut-short-by-ai/ -->

The authors of that brief are appropriately careful, and two of their caveats matter here. If government
R&D funding cuts in this period fell hardest on AI-exposed jobs, that would exaggerate the effect. And if
the AI start-up boom was simultaneously *creating* exposed jobs, the estimate understates what happens
when that stops.

It is a descriptive paper about a moving thing.

But put the two findings side by side and the shape of your actual risk becomes clear, and it is not "AI
will take my job." It is this:

**You are more likely to be in a job that AI complements. You are also less able to leave it.**

The IMF authors say it almost in passing: older and middle-aged workers have lower labour-market fluidity.
They change occupations, industries and locations less in response to shocks, and they take longer to find
work after losing it. Older workers are less able to adjust when disruptions occur, which the authors
note can leave them vulnerable precisely during rapid structural change — and which has a specific cost
when your time horizon for earning back a training investment is shorter than the person next to you's.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://pensionresearchcouncil.wharton.upenn.edu/blog/artificial-intelligence-and-older-workers-opportunity-risk-and-policy-tradeoffs/ -->

Read that next to Ch. 08 and something clicks.

The Stanford research found employment rising for workers aged 30 and over in exactly the exposed
occupations where it was falling for 22-to-25-year-olds. Same occupations. Opposite direction.

The difference is not exposure and it is not capability. It is that a 25-year-old who is displaced is at
the start of a search, and a 45-year-old is in the middle of one that they did not choose, with a
mortgage and a specific set of skills that fit one kind of organisation.

So the question this chapter has to answer is not "should I switch careers." It is narrower and much more
useful: **what makes a mid-career move expensive, and which of those costs can be paid down before you
need to move?**

## Why mid-career moves are expensive, and which costs you can pre-pay

There are three costs, and they behave completely differently. One is nearly unavoidable, one is cheaper
than people think, and one is the only one you can actually work on — and most people work on it only
after they need it.

### The real cost is the accumulated relationship

When economists study displaced workers, the number that keeps coming back is not about industries.

It is that the loss is largely the loss of **tenure** — the specific, unwritten, accumulated thing that
makes a particular person valuable in a particular place. That is why a laid-off worker's earnings recover
slowly even after the economy improves, and why the recovery is worse for people who had been somewhere
longer.

You do not need the literature to see this, though. You have fifteen years of it.

You know who to call when the vendor is being difficult. You know which of the three plausible approaches
will actually work in your organisation, because you have watched two of them fail. You know what your
manager will and will not defend you on.

None of that is on your résumé or in any course, and all of it is tied to *here*.

This cost is real and you cannot pre-pay it. An external move genuinely does burn it. Pretending otherwise
is how people end up doing the same job at a worse company for less money and calling it a career change.

But this is the cost that should change your *strategy*, not your *decision*. It is why the chapter argues,
later, for keeping a foot inside the structure you understand while you build something new outside it,
rather than torching the position and starting from zero.

### The cost of the credential is lower than it looks — and the test is not the interview

The second cost is the one that stops most people. Any search starts with a filter, and the filter is
usually a document.

That filter is measurably hostile to age in a way that is uncomfortable to read about and important to
know. The cleanest experimental evidence comes from a résumé audit study that sent matched,
computer-generated applications to **3,996** companies in two US cities, differing only in the implied age
of the applicant.

Women whose graduation dates implied they were 50, 55 or 62 needed **42% to 46% more applications** to get
an interview offer for an entry-level job than women whose dates implied 35 or 45. For every additional
decade of age, a woman in one city needed about five more applications to land one interview offer.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://gap.hks.harvard.edu/age-women-and-hiring-experimental-study -->

And the study's most useful finding is a negative one.

The researchers tested several résumé items designed to counteract the stereotype that older workers
resist new tools — a computer certification, a high-energy sport, an explicit willingness to embrace
change. **None of them produced a measurable effect.** The author also checked whether HR departments,
presumed better trained against bias, screened differently. They did not.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://gap.hks.harvard.edu/age-women-and-hiring-experimental-study -->

That is a 2008 result and it concerns entry-level hiring in two cities. Do not treat the exact percentages
as current.

Treat the shape as the finding, because it lines up with everything else in the book: when the filter is a
document, the document is where the arbitrage is, and **strengthening the document does not work**.

Certificates are documents. The thing that bypasses a document filter is not a better document. It is
somebody who already knows whether you can do the work.

Which means the cost of the credential is not paid at the interview. It is paid at the point where you
stop applying through the front door. The ZipRecruiter data is useful here: employers are still weighting
critical thinking above workflow automation, at **65%** against **60%**.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.ziprecruiter-research.org/economic-insights-research/ai-employer-report-2026 -->

That is an argument for a route that lets you demonstrate judgment before you are formally hired, since
the formal route is priced against you. Which brings us to the cost that is actually worth your time.

### The cost you can actually pre-pay is the one nobody starts early

If the first cost is the relationship and the second is the filter, the third is **the lag between
disruption and your ability to do something else.**

And the evidence says the intervention most people reach for first is close to the weakest one available.

The largest randomised evaluation of US public job training — more than **34,000** job seekers at over 200
American Job Centers, assigned to different service combinations — found that intensive staff assistance
*worked*, raising earnings by **$3,300 to $7,100** per person, a 7% to 20% gain, and paid for itself from
the taxpayer's side too.

Training did not. The evaluation's own summary says the evidence "is not conclusive" but "suggests that
training funded by the Adult and Dislocated Worker programs does not have positive impacts in the 30
months after study enrollment."

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.mathematica.org/projects/wia-gold-standard-evaluation -->

Be careful about what that does and does not say — the authors flag that only a minority of participants
were funded for training and that many in the control group found funding elsewhere, which dilutes the
comparison. It is not proof that training never works.

But it is the strongest evidence available on the question that mid-career switchers ask, and the honest
reading is that a generic course does not, by itself, change your earnings within two and a half years.

Contrast that with what the ASA data say older workers are actually doing, which is more interesting than
the "they don't use AI" headline suggests.

Only **23%** of workers aged 50 and over use AI to a great or some extent at work, against **73%** who use
it barely or not at all. But **49%** say they are interested in training, while only **12%** have had any.
And on LinkedIn Learning, the participation gap between older and younger workers has closed from **13.5
percentage points in 2022 to 1.6 points in 2025**.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://generations.asaging.org/not-left-behind-older-workers-artificial-intelligence-and-the-data-behind-adoption-and-adaptation/ -->

That is not resistance. That is unmet demand, in a population that is already quietly doing the work.

Which reframes the whole thing. The point is not to enrol in a course in case you need one. The point is
that the third cost — how long you are out of the water — is determined by whether you have already been
swimming.

So here is the chapter's actual claim, and it is deliberately modest and deliberately unsexy:

**Your judgment is the asset, and it was never the problem. The problem is that it is currently installed
in one place.**

Everything below is about the fact that the asset is genuinely worth what people say, and the fact that a
move which keeps it is a very different trade from a move which loses it.

| The three costs | Can you pre-pay it? | What actually reduces it |
|---|---|---|
| The accumulated relationship | No | Nothing does. Keep a way back in; do not torch the position. |
| The document filter | Not by improving the document | A route where someone already knows your work. Certificates did not move the audit-study result. |
| The lag to first income somewhere else | Yes, and it is the only one | Practice before you need it: paid work outside your job, while you still have the job |

## The transfer, not the leap

Now the practical part, and it rests on one distinction that the three costs above make unavoidable.

A **leap** keeps none of the assets. New industry, new skill, no relationship, no track record, and a
search that runs through the filter that is priced against you.

That is the shape most people picture when they say "career change," and it is the shape the evidence is
worst for — including the part of the evidence that says the training you would do to prepare for it
probably does not pay back inside two and a half years.

A **transfer** keeps the tacit asset and changes the container it is used in. Same judgment, different
problem; same people who know you can be trusted with the hard call, different thing being called hard.
The exposure may change completely. What does not change is that somebody already knows how you handle a
decision that costs money if you get it wrong.

Ch. 05's distinction is the test for which one you are planning.

Codified knowledge — what is written down, teachable, checkable — is the part AI substitutes for, and
therefore the part that does not transfer because it is not yours to transfer anyway. Tacit knowledge is
what practice taught you and nobody wrote down.

The transfer is: take the tacit half to a new set of tasks and let the codified half be handled by the
tools.

Concretely, that means the unit to change is **the problem you are pointed at**, not the industry you are
labelled with. Three questions decide whether a specific move is a transfer or a leap:

**Does anything I already know make me better at this on day one?** Not "is it interesting." Not "will it
grow." Better. If the honest answer is no, you are planning a leap, and you should know that you are
paying full price.

**Will the new work let me be wrong in public and be seen recovering?** This is the one people skip.
Judgment is not demonstrated by a certificate, and the audit study shows the certificate does not change
the screening. It is demonstrated by having been responsible for an outcome someone else observed. If a
move offers no venue for that in the first year, it is offering you a wage and not a position.

**Who is the smallest number of people who would have to say yes?** Mid-career transfers almost never
happen through a process. They happen because one person who has seen your work says a name. The
whatever-size-of-company question matters less than whether that person exists yet, which is why this is a
thing to build before you need it rather than a thing to search for once you do.

That last point is where the evidence about *where older workers actually are* turns into something
actionable, and it is the happiest finding in this chapter.

LinkedIn's data put **49.4%** of workers aged 50 and over in roles considered insulated from generative AI
disruption, against **42.2%** of younger workers. And the composition of what older workers are learning
has shifted toward technology topics, from **19.5%** to **26.6%** of their learning sessions between 2022
and 2025.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://generations.asaging.org/not-left-behind-older-workers-artificial-intelligence-and-the-data-behind-adoption-and-adaptation/ -->

Nobody is asking you to become a different person.

The population you belong to is already in the better-protected half of the labour market, already
learning, and already under-served by the training on offer. The move is not to abandon your judgment for
a new skill. It is to make your judgment **portable** — which happens in small paid increments, on work
you can point at, with people who were not obliged to give you the chance.

And there is an urgency argument that has nothing to do with AI, which is the one worth ending on.

The market you are transferring into and the market you would be leap-frogging into are not the same shape.
Senior postings on Indeed rose **14.7%** year over year, while entry-level postings fell **7.5%**. Only
about **14%** of all postings are senior roles against **40%** mid-level.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://hiringlab.indeed.com/2026/07/23/the-labor-market-is-tilting-toward-seniority/ -->

You are not competing with the 22-year-old for the entry-level rung. You would lose that, and you should
not enter it.

You are competing for the mid-level and senior roles where your tacit knowledge is the product — the
market that is growing, in the sense that it is shrinking less — and where the certificate was never going
to be the thing that decided it.

## The honest caveats

**The two headline papers do not agree, and I have not resolved them.** The IMF authors find older workers
better positioned; the Center for Retirement Research finds them leaving work faster. The chapter's
reconciliation — same exposure, lower fluidity — is my reading of both, not a finding either paper states.
If a better synthesis appears, it replaces this one.

**The CRR result is descriptive and its authors say so.** If government R&D cuts in the period hit
AI-exposed jobs hardest, the estimated effect is exaggerated; if the AI boom was creating those jobs at
the same time, it is understated. Either way it is one brief, on one country, for one technology
transition, and it is early.

**The age-discrimination evidence is old and narrow.** The audit study is from 2008, covers entry-level
hiring for women in two US cities, and does not measure what happens at the seniority level you are
actually transacting in. What travels is the mechanism — a document-based filter resists document-based
fixes — not the 42% figure.

**The strongest evidence against the chapter's own advice is a paper I could not open.** Economic Mobility
Corporation's six-year follow-up on sectoral training programmes found earnings impacts that *grew* over
time, from around $2,300 in year three to $5,080 in year six. That is a real counter to the "training does
not pay" reading, and the distinction that probably matters is sectoral, employer-linked training versus
generic courses. I could not extract the numbers myself, so it is recorded in the notes as the first thing
a revision should chase rather than folded in here.

**The whole chapter is a US labour market.** Every figure is American: CPS, ADP, Indeed, ZipRecruiter, the
WIA evaluation, the Boston College brief. The mechanism may travel; the magnitudes will not.

**And the caveat that matters most.** If you are already out — laid off, six months in, watching the
savings — this chapter is describing a position you no longer have. The advice to build the transfer before
you need it is advice for someone with a job, and it is not helpful to be told it late. In that situation,
do not start with a course. Start with the smallest paid version of the work you have already done for
someone who already knows you, because that is the only one of the three costs that moves quickly.

## Do this today

1. **Under 30 minutes.** Write down the three costs against your own situation, one line each: what you
   would actually lose if you left, how you would currently reach a hiring manager, and how long you
   could go without income. Most people discover the third number is the one that decides everything,
   and it is the one they have never written down.
2. **This week.** Pick one thing you are already good at that someone outside your organisation would
   pay for, and find the smallest possible paid version of it — a review, an audit, a two-hour consult,
   a fixed-scope piece of work. Charge for it. The point is not the money. It is that a stranger's
   payment is the one signal that does not go through the filter that is priced against you.
3. **This quarter.** Make one transfer real and keep the leap theoretical. One new problem, the same
   judgment, at least one person outside your current employer who has now seen you handle it. And do
   not quit anything yet — the relationship you would burn is the one asset the evidence says you cannot
   rebuild quickly, and it is worth more intact than as a story about courage.
