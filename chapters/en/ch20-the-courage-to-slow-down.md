# 20. The Courage to Slow Down

The World Health Organization and the International Labour Organization did the first global accounting of
what long working hours cost in lives.

In 2016, the figure was **745,000 deaths** from stroke and ischaemic heart disease — a **29% increase since
2000**.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.who.int/news/item/17-05-2021-long-working-hours-increasing-deaths-from-heart-disease-and-stroke-who-ilo -->

Working **55 or more hours a week** is associated with a **35% higher risk of stroke** and a **17% higher
risk of dying from heart disease**, compared with a 35-to-40-hour week.

Long hours are now the occupational risk factor with the largest disease burden, and the number of people
working them is rising — **9% of the global population**.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.who.int/news/item/17-05-2021-long-working-hours-increasing-deaths-from-heart-disease-and-stroke-who-ilo -->

That is not a metaphor about burnout. It is a body count, assembled from 37 studies covering more than
768,000 participants for heart disease and 22 studies covering more than 839,000 for stroke.

> **The one thing to take away:** slowing down is not a reward you grant yourself after the work is
> finished. The work does not finish, and the evidence says the delay is expensive in a currency you cannot
> earn back.

## What the acceleration actually costs

Begin with the accounting, because the cultural conversation treats overwork as a character flaw in one
direction and a virtue in the other, and the research treats it as neither. It treats it as an exposure.

The WHO/ILO numbers are the clearest version. A 35% increase in stroke risk is in the range of risks people
routinely reorganise their lives to avoid, and it arrives attached to something many people are praised
for.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.who.int/news/item/17-05-2021-long-working-hours-increasing-deaths-from-heart-disease-and-stroke-who-ilo -->

And the costs are not only medical.

The paper that frames the field — Giurge, Whillans and West in *Nature Human Behaviour* — makes an argument
that has become the standard way of talking about this. Global wealth rose substantially over two decades,
and **material affluence did not translate into time affluence**. Most people report persistent time
poverty: the chronic sense of having too many things to do and not enough time to do them. And time poverty,
the authors report, is linked to lower well-being, worse physical health and lower productivity.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://wellbeing.hmc.ox.ac.uk/publications/why-time-poverty-matters-for-individuals-organisations-and-nations/ -->

The single most arresting finding in that work is a comparison worth quoting precisely, because it reframes
the whole trade. In an analysis of **2.5 million Americans, the subjective feeling of time poverty had a
stronger negative effect on well-being than being unemployed**.

That comparison is doing enormous work, and it is worth being careful with, because the study is a
Perspective and the analysis is cross-sectional — which is the chapter's main caveat, and I will come back
to it.

But take it at face value for a moment and see what it does to the standard defence of overwork. That
defence is always some version of: this is hard now, but it buys security later. If time poverty is worse
for your well-being than not having a job at all, then the trade is not comfort-now against
security-later. One side of it is *worse than unemployment*, which is not a position anyone would choose
deliberately.

A recent scoping review reaches the same direction from the other end: higher perceived time affluence is
associated with greater psychological well-being, lower stress and higher life satisfaction — while noting
that the field measures the construct inconsistently enough that comparisons across studies are shaky.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2026.1824268/full -->

So the first section's answer is that acceleration is not free, the price is not only emotional, and the
thing most people believe they are trading for — future security — is purchased with a present that is
measurably bad for them.

## What money does and does not buy

Now the question that decides whether any of this is actionable, and the one where most advice on this
subject is out of date: does earning less actually make you happier?

For fifteen years, the popular answer has been a single number. Kahneman and Deaton's 2010 study found that
emotional well-being rose with income and then **flattened at around $75,000 a year**, while life evaluation
kept rising. That finding became the licence for a whole genre of advice: past a certain point, more money
does not help, so stop chasing it.

The problem is that the finding was not quite right, and the people who could fix it were the people who
disagreed about it. Kahneman's result and Killingsworth's contrary 2021 finding were put together in an
adversarial collaboration — the two authors and a facilitator, jointly reanalysing the data to see which of
them had made the error.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10013834/ -->

The resolution is more interesting than either original claim, and it is worth stating exactly.

The flattening is **real, but it is restricted to the least happy 20% of people**, whose happiness rises
with income up to roughly **$100,000** and then stops. For the other 80%, happiness keeps rising with income
— and in the happiest group it **accelerates** above that level.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10013834/ -->

The authors also identified where the original confusion came from, and it is a lesson in reading research
that goes well beyond this chapter.

Kahneman and Deaton asked people whether they had experienced specific feelings the previous day, and scored
them as fractions — which means their measure of "happiness" was arguably a measure of **unhappiness**,
sitting near the top of its range for almost everyone. As the authors put it, if the scale had been labelled
"unhappiness", the plateau would have been correctly identified as applying to a category of unhappy people.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10013834/ -->

That matters for this chapter because it removes an easy excuse in both directions.

You cannot say "money stops mattering at $75,000, so I should work less" — for most people it does not stop
mattering. And you cannot say "money keeps helping, so the hours are worth it" — because the WHO data says
the hours have their own price, and the price is not denominated in money.

The honest position is that the two are genuinely in tension and the resolution does not dissolve it. For
most people, more income does buy more experienced happiness. And long hours buy strokes. The trade is real,
it does not have a clever answer, and the point of this chapter is that most people are making it by default
rather than on purpose.

## Why a holiday does not fix it

Here is the finding that stops this becoming a rest-and-you-will-be-fine essay, and it is the reason the
chapter has a practical section rather than a lifestyle recommendation.

Vacations work. A meta-analysis of 22 studies found significant post-vacation reductions in exhaustion,
improvements in mood and increases in life satisfaction, and a later review of 54 studies found
physiological improvements too — lower cortisol, better heart-rate variability, improved sleep.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12334972/ -->

And then they stop working, quickly. In one study, 54 employees were monitored through vacations averaging
23 days. Health and well-being **peaked around the eighth day** and returned to pre-vacation levels **within
a week of going back to work**.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12334972/ -->

Read that again, because it has an uncomfortable implication. Peak recovery arrived at day eight of a 23-day
holiday, and the entire benefit was gone seven days after returning. A long vacation is not a larger dose of
a good thing. It is a plateau followed by a cliff.

The same literature points at what does work better: **frequency**. Employees who take multiple short breaks
through the year show higher energy and motivation than those who take one long one, and the recommended
spacing is a short break roughly every two months.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12334972/ -->

Which produces the chapter's real argument, and it is not the one people expect.

The problem is not that you are not resting. It is that you are returning to the same structure, and the
structure regenerates the strain faster than the rest removes it. A holiday is a temporary change to the
inputs. The fade-out is the system reverting to its equilibrium.

If that is right, then the answer cannot be "take more holidays", and it cannot be "rest more efficiently"
either. It has to be a change to the structure that produces the strain in the first place.

### Why this takes courage rather than discipline

The chapter's title says courage, which is a strong word for a decision about working hours, so it is worth
being specific about what the courage is for.

There are real costs to slowing down, and the honest version of this chapter has to name them rather than
treat them as illusions.

If your industry rewards visible availability, reducing it has consequences. If your peers are still at
their desks, leaving has a social price. If the thing you are paid for is a competition, choosing to run
less hard changes where you finish. Nobody needs a study to know that people who work less are sometimes
passed over, and a chapter that claimed otherwise would be selling something.

But the costs divide into two parts, and only one of them is as large as it feels.

The exaggerated part is the sense that everyone else is doing more. The WHO data puts the share of people
working 55 or more hours a week at **9% globally** — which is a large number of people and a small fraction
of workers.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.who.int/news/item/17-05-2021-long-working-hours-increasing-deaths-from-heart-disease-and-stroke-who-ilo -->

Extreme hours are a minority practice that the culture represents as the norm, for the simple reason that
the people doing them are the ones with something to say about what a career looks like. Your social media
feed is not a sample. Neither, for that matter, is the leadership of most companies, which is drawn
disproportionately from people who chose to work that way and for whom it happened to work.

The real part is narrower and harder to argue with: in some specific roles, at specific firms, in specific
years, visible overwork genuinely is the price of admission. That is not a fiction and it is not solved by an
essay.

The useful question is not "is this fair", to which the answer is no and which leads nowhere, but "which of
these costs are real, which am I imagining, and for the real ones, is the price one I am choosing to pay?"

The difference between a person who works 60 hours because their career requires it and a person who works
60 hours because they have never checked is not the hours. It is whether the decision was made.

### Why the tools will not do this for you

One more thing, because this book has spent most of its length arguing that the machine changes what work
is. It would be easy to conclude that the machine will also fix the hours. It will not, at least not on its
own, and the WHO's own framing is the clearest evidence.

The report notes that the pandemic accelerated the trend toward longer working time, and it names the
mechanism: teleworking became normal, **"often blurring the boundaries between home and work"**, and where
businesses scaled back, **the people still on the payroll ended up working longer hours**.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.who.int/news/item/17-05-2021-long-working-hours-increasing-deaths-from-heart-disease-and-stroke-who-ilo -->

That is the pattern to expect, and it is worth holding onto precisely because it contradicts the intuitive
version.

A technology that makes output cheaper does not automatically hand the difference back as time. It changes
the expectations around it first. If a report took a day and now takes two hours, the plausible outcomes are
that you produce four times as much, or that the deadline moves, or that the work expands to include things
that were previously not worth anyone's time.

"You finish at three" is not on that list unless somebody decides it is.

The historical version of this is well known in other domains. The four-day week argument has been made
since at least the 1930s, on the reasoning that productivity gains would be converted into leisure, and the
conversion has never happened automatically. It happens when it is negotiated, legislated, or decided by
someone with the authority to decide it.

Which means the hours you get are the hours that were allocated, not the hours that were left over. And
"left over" is exactly the category the rest of this book spent fourteen chapters teaching you to eliminate.

## What slowing down actually requires

So what does that mean concretely, given that for most readers the working hours are not entirely theirs to
set?

The honest answer starts with a division of the problem, because "slow down" as a single instruction is
useless.

**The hours you control.** Some of your time is genuinely yours to allocate, and Ch. 19's advice applies
directly — protect the hours that produce nothing.

What this chapter adds is why that protection keeps failing. It fails because the unbounded task expands to
fill whatever you give it, so an hour "saved" is immediately claimed by work rather than kept. The defence
is not willpower. It is that the hours need to be committed to something else with a claim on them, which is
why Ch. 17's recurring obligations turn out to be doing double duty again: a standing commitment is a
structural block on the calendar, and it does not require you to decide in the moment.

**The hours you do not control.** This is the uncomfortable half, and the WHO data is the leverage. If your
employer's expectations put you above 55 hours regularly, the evidence says that is a health exposure with a
measurable risk attached, not a busy season.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.who.int/news/item/17-05-2021-long-working-hours-increasing-deaths-from-heart-disease-and-stroke-who-ilo -->

You may not be able to change it, and this chapter is not going to pretend that a book can. But there is a
difference between a constraint you have accepted knowingly and one you have absorbed as normal, and Ch.
14's framework applies: what is this actually costing, what would it take to change it, and what is the plan
if you do not.

**The boundary between them, which is where the leverage is.** Most people have more room than they believe,
in a specific place: the boundary is much softer than it looks.

"I will be offline after seven" is a structural decision. "I will try to finish earlier" is not, because it
has no edge and can be overridden by any urgent thing. The difference between the two is not effort. It is
whether the commitment is specific enough to be broken.

And one thing that does not work, worth naming because it is the most attractive option. **You cannot bank
the rest.** Twenty-three days of vacation produce peak recovery on day eight and nothing a week later — which
means the instinct to work flat out now in order to rest properly later is not a strategy. It is one of the
mechanisms by which the strain accumulates.

## The honest caveats

**The most striking number in the chapter is the weakest design.** The comparison between time poverty and
unemployment — the one about 2.5 million Americans — comes from a Perspective paper, and the analysis is
cross-sectional. People who are unhappy may perceive themselves as more time-poor, which would produce the
same correlation with the arrow pointing the other way. The chapter reports it because it reframes the
trade, and it should not be quoted as though it were causal.

**The income finding is a resolution and it is still not advice.** Killingsworth, Kahneman and Mellers
settled what the earlier studies disagreed about. They did not establish that working more hours to earn
more money increases happiness, because hours have their own effect and the two studies did not measure it
jointly. Do not read the resolution as permission to chase income.

**The whole chapter is about working hours, and many readers do not choose them.** The WHO figures are about
exposure, most of which is set by employers, industries and economies. A chapter in a book cannot change
that, and framing the problem as a personal decision would be dishonest about where the power actually sits.

**And the fade-out finding has two readings, only one of which implies the chapter's advice.** It could mean
that rest does not accumulate — or it could mean that returning to *the same job* undoes it. If the second is
true, then the people whose work is the problem should change the work, and everyone else should take more
frequent breaks. Nobody has run the study that separates those, and the recommendation here leans on the
interpretation that fits the rest of the chapter's evidence.

**Finally: slowing down is a trade, and I have not pretended it is free.** The income evidence says most
people do get happier as they earn more. The overwork evidence says the hours have a price. Those two things
point in opposite directions, and a chapter that resolved them tidily would be lying about the shape of the
decision.

## Do this today

1. **Under 30 minutes.** Write down your actual hours for the last two weeks — not the contracted ones, the
   real ones, including the evenings. Then compare the number to 55. Most people have never done this
   arithmetic, and some will find they have been above the WHO's risk line for years without noticing it as
   a health fact rather than a work ethic.
2. **This week.** Convert one soft intention into a hard edge: "I will try to stop earlier" becomes "I am
   offline after seven on Tuesdays and Thursdays." The test is whether somebody else could tell you had
   broken it. If not, it is not a boundary, it is a hope.
3. **This quarter.** Replace one long break with several short ones, spaced roughly every two months, and
   make them commitments rather than options. The evidence is that frequency outperforms duration, and that
   the benefit of a single long vacation is gone within a week of returning — which means the rest you have
   been deferring is not accumulating in the meantime.
