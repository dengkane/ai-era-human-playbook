# 21. Designing the Life You Actually Want

Two researchers spent years asking people what they regret most, and found a pattern that is now well
replicated. When people look back over a **short** period, their regrets centre on things they **did**.
When they look back over a **life**, the regrets shift to things they **did not do** — mentioned
significantly more often than regrets of action.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10282588/ -->

The mechanism is specific, and it is not flattering. Regrets of inaction persist because people
**romanticise the road not taken** — exaggerating all the good things that would have followed — and
because the worries that stopped them at the time are forgotten.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10282588/ -->

> **The one thing to take away:** the version of your life you will have is the one you defaulted into,
> unless something interrupts it. That is not a motivational claim. It is what the regret research and
> the status quo bias both say, and they say it in the same direction.

## What people actually regret, and why

Begin with the content, because "you'll regret what you didn't do" is the sort of sentence that has been
on a poster somewhere, and it deserves to be replaced with something checkable.

A meta-analysis pooled **11 studies that ranked people's regrets** and found the six biggest, in
descending order: **education, career, romance, parenting, the self, and leisure**.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2394712/ -->

Look at that list for a moment, because it is not what a self-help book would predict. The top regret is
education — not "I studied the wrong thing" but, overwhelmingly, "I should have studied more", "I should
have got another degree". Career is second. Neither is about a risk taken that went wrong.

And the explanation the researchers reach for is the most useful idea in this chapter. They call it the
**opportunity principle**: greater perceived opportunity produces *more intense* regret, because when
opportunity is foreclosed, people engage in rationalisation and the regret dissolves.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2394712/ -->

Which explains both the ranking and the mechanism in one move. Education tops the list because, in a
society where it is always possible to go back and learn something, the door never quite closes — so the
regret never gets resolved. Parenting ranks fourth for the same reason in one direction and the opposite
in another: it is the domain people most often describe as the one they cannot redo.

Read together, the two findings flip the standard framing. The thing to fear is not failure. Failure is
an outcome, and outcomes get rationalised, explained and absorbed — which is precisely why regrets of
action fade and regrets of inaction do not.

The thing to fear is the door you never tried, because it stays open enough to keep costing you something
and closed enough that you cannot do anything about it.

## Why the default wins, and why that is the whole problem

If the regret data says "act", the obvious question is why people do not. The answer is not laziness, and
it is worth being precise, because every chapter of advice in this book has been pushing against the same
current.

The current is that **the default is not neutral**. Maintaining the current situation demands less
cognitive effort and carries less psychological risk than changing it, and the perceived costs of a change
weigh more heavily than the expected gains — even when the gains objectively outweigh them. Overcoming
that has a name in the literature: the activation barrier.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11018899/ -->

Two things make that barrier taller than it looks, and this book has spent twenty chapters on both.

The first is that you will rarely feel like it. The evidence on what actually drives people — the
autonomy to choose your own action, the competence to do it, and the connection to other people — says
these are not rewards you get for changing, they are conditions that make change possible.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12887546/ -->

The second is that a great deal of the work of Ch. 05–15 was about making your present position stronger:
a better role, a more defensible price, a portfolio of clients, a tested judgement. Every one of those is
a reason to stay, and the stronger you made it, the higher the barrier to leaving — even when leaving is
what you would choose.

So the honest picture is not that people fail to design their lives out of weakness. It is that a life
which is working adequately produces a powerful, rational force in favour of continuing it, and the
regret data is the only evidence available about what that costs over decades.

And there is one more piece of evidence worth having, because it locates the problem precisely. In 2025,
across 138 countries, a median of **82% of adults** said they were satisfied with their freedom to choose
what to do with their life.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://news.gallup.com/poll/710513/people-worldwide-satisfied-freedom-life.aspx -->

Most people, most places, report having the freedom. Which means the constraint is not usually the one
people name when they explain why they have not done the thing. It is the barrier, not the cage.

## The smallest thing that actually works

Here is where the research becomes more useful than the motivation industry, and more modest.

Researchers ran a randomised trial with **476 participants** to test a specific, small intervention. The
procedure was: choose a life area you value, affirm why it matters, identify **one concrete action**
related to it, plan when you would do it, and carry it out within the following week.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11018899/ -->

The results, against both an affirmation-only group and a no-treatment control: significant gains in
**self-insight**, **sense of coherence**, and **reduced symptoms of psychopathology**, measured at one
and two weeks.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11018899/ -->

And then, at three weeks, the effects were no longer significant.

That last sentence is the most important one in the chapter, and it is the reason this chapter is not
going to tell you that one decision changes everything. One action is one action. The effect was real, it
was measurable, and it decayed.

But look at what the participants actually chose, because it tells you a great deal about what "designing
your life" looks like in practice. The most common category was not career or money. It was **family
relationships** — a third of all choices. Then physical activity, health, romantic relationships. Career
and finances came fifth. And among the things people reported as enabling them to follow through, the
top answer was **planning and reminders**.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11018899/ -->

So the picture the evidence supports is not a grand plan. It is a small loop, repeated: pick something
you actually value, convert it into one concrete action, schedule it so it happens without a decision,
do it, and then run the loop again — because the effect from the last one will have worn off.

That is unglamorous, and it is also consistent with everything in this book. Ch. 10 said measure rather
than assume. Ch. 15 said test on your own work. Ch. 17 and Ch. 20 both said that the things requiring a
standing commitment survive and the things requiring willpower do not. This is the same finding arriving
at the last chapter: the loop is small, it is scheduled, and it is re-run.

## The whole book, in one page

This is the last chapter, so it is worth saying what the preceding twenty add up to, in the order a
person would actually need them.

**Face reality.** AI is not a tool with a manual; it is a species with a trajectory, and your instincts
about it are calibrated to a map that has been redrawn (Ch. 01–02). The limits worth building on are the
structural ones — verification, accountability — not the ones that expire when a model improves (Ch. 03).
That is the entire foundation: not "AI can't", but "here is what does not change".

**Secure the baseline.** Start where you are (Ch. 04). Inside a job, the part of your work that is
written down is the part being absorbed first, and the tacit half is the asset (Ch. 05). If you own
something, small is newly viable and mostly underused (Ch. 06). If you sell your time, sell a unit that
isn't hours (Ch. 07). If you are choosing, three questions outlast the tooling (Ch. 08); if you are
switching, transfer the judgment rather than leaping (Ch. 09).

**Amplify your leverage.** Find your own frontier by testing it (Ch. 10), curate what the model sees
(Ch. 11), build a business where the second sale is cheaper than the first (Ch. 12), and stop competing
on output, because the machine wins and the market has noticed (Ch. 13). Then decide whether to go
independent on the numbers, not the story (Ch. 14), and test your tools on your own work rather than on
a leaderboard (Ch. 15).

**Beyond survival.** Generation is cheap and discrimination is not, so the part of creativity that is
yours is the choosing (Ch. 16). Relationships are a health input, not a nice-to-have, and the structure
protects you more than any individual conversation (Ch. 17). When what you did stops being needed, what
you were needed *for* is what has to be rebuilt (Ch. 18).

**And then the part the machine cannot reach.** Some experiences lose their value the moment they are
mediated, because they were never about the output (Ch. 19). The hours you get are the ones you allocated,
not the ones left over (Ch. 20). And the life you end up with is the one you defaulted into, unless
something interrupts it — which is what this chapter's evidence says, and what the small loop is for.

Nowhere in those twenty chapters is there a claim that any of it is easy, or that the constraints are
imaginary. A great many readers are not choosing their hours, do not control their working conditions,
and are not in a position to restructure anything this year. The book has tried to be honest about that,
which is why it keeps returning to structural moves — a standing commitment, a schedule, a recorded
decision, a second source of being-needed — rather than to mindset.

And the one thing it has been consistent about, from the first chapter to this one, is the direction of
the test. Not "what can AI not do", which has an expiry date, but "if this limit disappeared, would it be
because a model got better, or because people decided something?" The first kind you plan around. The
second kind you get to argue about — and those arguments, about what needs a person on the other end, are
the ones where you have a vote.

### Three scales, not one plan

"Designing your life" sounds like a document. Something you produce once, feel briefly good about, and
then file. The evidence in this chapter describes something else, and it is worth making the structure
explicit because the difference is what makes it survivable.

There are three scales, and they run on different clocks.

**The week.** The action. Pick a value, convert it to one concrete thing, schedule it, do it. The trial
measured this at one and two weeks and found effects on coherence and self-insight — and found the effect
gone by three, which tells you the clock on the smallest scale.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11018899/ -->

**The quarter.** The structure. This is where the moves from the rest of the book live: the boundary you
made hard, the standing obligation you took on, the second source of being-needed, the replacement of one
long break with several short ones. These do not produce a feeling. They change what happens by default,
so that the weekly loop is not fighting the same current every time.

**The year, or something like it.** The re-diagnosis. Ch. 04 opened the book by asking where you actually
stand, and its answer was a snapshot rather than a verdict. The honest use of it is to run it again — the
same questions, twelve months later, and notice which answers moved. That is the only mechanism in this
book for catching a life that is drifting while all three clocks are working.

The reason to describe it this way rather than as a plan is that the plan version fails in a specific,
predictable way. A plan is a large single decision, so it attracts the activation barrier at its worst —
it is the change whose perceived costs loom largest, and it is the one whose success depends on a
projection about a future self that will not have any of your current information. The three-scale
version is small enough to start with, structural enough to hold, and — the part that matters — it assumes
you will be wrong and provides for correcting.

Which is also the answer to a fair objection. Every one of the weekly actions might be the wrong one. You
may spend a quarter building a structure around something you turn out not to care about. But the regret
data says the cost of that is bounded: you will have a disappointment, rationalise it, and revise — and
revising is available precisely because the door was not closed.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2394712/ -->

### What this book could not do

It could not tell you what to want, and it would be worth being clear that this was a decision rather
than an omission.

Twenty-one chapters have tried to establish what is true about the terrain, what is measurable about the
tools, and what the evidence says about the moves available. None of that adds up to a recommendation
about your life, because the inputs are your numbers: your obligations, your health, your savings, your
tolerance for a bad year, the people who depend on you. A book that supplied an answer would be replacing
your judgement with its own, which is — in the argument this book has been making since Ch. 03 — the one
thing that is actually scarce and cannot be delegated.

So what it offers instead is a set of things you can run yourself. A test for whether a limit will expire
(Ch. 03). Three questions for a path (Ch. 08). A loop for finding your own frontier (Ch. 10). A method for
judging a tool without trusting a leaderboard (Ch. 15). A distinction between reusing judgement and
otherwise (Ch. 09). A small procedure for turning a value into an action, with a measured effect and a
measured decay (this chapter).

Each of those is deliberately something you can check. That was the constraint throughout: no claim that
required you to trust the author, because the author is a model-assisted text and the whole book has been
about what that does and does not entitle you to believe.

Which leaves the last point, and the one worth ending on. The thing this book is most confident about is
also the least comforting: the version of your life that arrives is the one you default into, the default
is set by forces that are not neutral, and the evidence about what that costs over decades is unusually
clear. The doors stay open, the regrets attach to the ones you never tried, and the path not taken gets
more romantic every year precisely because you never found out what was on it.

That is not a comfortable place to stop. It is, as far as the research goes, the accurate one.

## The honest caveats

**The regret ranking is American data.** Roese's meta-analysis pools US studies, and the replication ran
in a Chicago museum. The temporal pattern — recent regrets about action, long-term regrets about inaction
— has broader support, but the specific ranking of life domains should be read as one culture's answer to
a universal question.

**The opportunity principle is a strong claim from a mixed literature.** It fits education ranking first,
and the paper tests it in the lab. It is also the kind of explanation that is hard to falsify, since
"rationalisation dissolves regret" predicts that whatever people do not regret had low opportunity. Treat
it as the best available account rather than as a mechanism you can rely on quantitatively.

**The intervention's effect decayed, and the chapter says so rather than rounding it up.** Gains were
significant at one and two weeks and not at three. Whether that reflects a real decay or a study
underpowered at its final follow-up, I cannot tell you, and neither can the authors. It is why the
recommendation is a loop rather than a decision.

**And the whole framing of "designing a life" is contestable in a way the research cannot settle.** There
is a real argument that treating a life as a design problem is a modern, individualist posture, and that
most good lives are inherited, stumbled into, constrained by obligations, or simply lived rather than
planned. This chapter's position is narrow: the default is not neutral, and the regret data says the
inaction is what costs — so some deliberation is warranted. That is not the same as saying a life can be
engineered, and anyone selling you the second thing is selling.

**Finally, the book ends where it began, with a limit you should not overestimate.** Everything here
assumes a reader with enough room to make some choices. If you do not have that room, most of this is
describing a position you are not in — and the honest thing is to say that plainly rather than to imply
that the barrier was only ever in your head.

## Do this today

1. **Under 30 minutes.** Write the one sentence you would not want to be true at seventy: the thing you
   are not doing, that you could start, and that you would regret not having tried. Not a goal. A
   candidate for the first line of a list that the regret research says will otherwise be about exactly
   this. Then read it once and put it somewhere you will see it.
2. **This week.** Run the intervention the trial actually tested. Pick one area you value, affirm why,
   choose **one concrete action**, put it in the calendar with a reminder, and do it — this week, not
   eventually. The evidence for this is small and real, and the effect measured was on coherence and
   self-insight rather than on happiness, so do not go looking for a mood change.
3. **This quarter, and then again next quarter.** Run the loop a second time, and notice what your first
   round told you. The trial found that the top enabler was planning, and the top life area was family.
   Neither is what most people would guess in advance, which is the argument for running the thing rather
   than thinking about it — you find out what you actually value by watching what you choose, not by
   introspecting about it.

- [Richardson & Gilovich — *A very public replication of the temporal pattern to people's regrets*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10282588/)
  — open access, and a model of how to publish a replication. The mechanism section on romanticising the
  road not taken is the part worth your time.
- [Roese & Summerville — *What We Regret Most … and Why*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2394712/)
  — the meta-analysis and the opportunity principle. Read it for the ranking, stay for the explanation.
- [Russo-Netzer & Atad — *Activating values intervention*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11018899/)
  — the trial this chapter's "do this today" is modelled on, including the three-week result that keeps
  the chapter honest.
- Ch. 04 of this book, *Where Different People Actually Stand* — the diagnosis to re-run now that you have
  read the rest, because a surprising amount of it will read differently.
- Ch. 18 of this book, *Rebuilding Meaning* — what to do when the thing you were needed for changes,
  which is the question most likely to bring you back to this chapter.
- Ch. 20 of this book, *The Courage to Slow Down* — the hours this chapter assumes you have, and what to
  do if you do not.
